## The Bird of Paradise

Apus is a small, faint constellation huddled near the south celestial pole, never visible
from most of the Northern Hemisphere. Its name comes from the Greek for "without feet" —
a strange misunderstanding about one of the most beautiful birds on Earth.

## History

Apus is not an ancient constellation and carries no myth. Its stars were charted in the
1590s by the Dutch navigators Pieter Dirkszoon Keyser and Frederick de Houtman during a
trading voyage to the East Indies, and the figure first appeared on a celestial globe
published by Petrus Plancius in 1598. Johann Bayer included it in his atlas *Uranometria*
in 1603, and it has stayed on charts ever since.

The name records a European mistake. Skins of New Guinea birds of paradise reached Europe
with the feet removed, and the traders who sold them claimed the birds never landed at all
but floated permanently in the air. From the Greek *apous*, "footless," the constellation
became the footless bird — a creature invented by bad taxidermy and credulous buyers.

## Notable stars

- **Alpha Apodis** — the brightest star in the constellation at magnitude 3.8, an orange
  giant about 410 light-years away. Even at its brightest, Apus is a dim figure.
- **Gamma Apodis** — magnitude 3.9 and only a little over 160 light-years from us, a
  yellowish star slightly warmer than our Sun's surface.
- **Beta Apodis** — magnitude 4.2, at a very similar distance of about 158 light-years,
  though the two are unrelated neighbours seen by chance along the same line of sight.
- **Delta-1 Apodis** — a deep red giant of magnitude 4.7, with a surface temperature
  around 2750 K. It forms a wide pair with Delta-2 Apodis, splittable in binoculars.

## How to find it

Apus is a Southern Hemisphere constellation, best placed on winter evenings around July,
and it needs a dark sky. Start from Alpha and Beta Centauri, follow them south to the
compact triangle of Triangulum Australe, then continue in the same direction toward the
pole — Apus lies just beyond, between Triangulum Australe and the polar constellation
Octans.

Do not expect a bird shape. What you will find is a thin scatter of fourth-magnitude stars,
worth tracking down for the story rather than the view.
