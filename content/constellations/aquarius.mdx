## The Water Bearer

Aquarius is a sprawling, faint constellation depicting a young man pouring water from a
jar. Despite its size and ancient pedigree, none of its stars are particularly bright,
and tracing the figure takes a dark sky and patience.

## Mythology

In Greek myth, Aquarius is Ganymede, a Trojan prince so beautiful that Zeus, in the form
of an eagle, swept him up to Olympus to serve as cupbearer to the gods. He was given
immortality and placed among the stars, eternally pouring out the divine waters.

The water-pouring figure is far older than the Greeks. In Babylonian astronomy, this
region of the sky was the Great One — a god holding an overflowing vase, source of the
rivers of the world. Egyptian astronomers connected the constellation's position with the
annual flooding of the Nile. In ancient times the constellations Aquarius, Pisces,
Capricornus, Cetus, and Eridanus all sat together in a watery quarter of the sky known as
**the Sea**.

## Notable stars and objects

- **Sadalsuud** — "the luckiest of the lucky," a yellow supergiant about 540 light-years
  away. Its name reflects an ancient belief that its rising marked the start of a season
  of mild rains.
- **Sadalmelik** — "the lucky one of the king," another yellow supergiant.
- **The Helix Nebula** (NGC 7293) — one of the closest planetary nebulae to Earth, the
  glowing remains of a dying Sun-like star. Sometimes called the "Eye of God" for its
  haunting telescope photographs.

## How to find it

Aquarius is best viewed in autumn evenings, low in the southern sky. Find the Great
Square of Pegasus first, then look down and to the south — Aquarius lies just below it.
Look for the small Y-shaped asterism called the **Water Jar**, three stars marking the
mouth of Ganymede's pitcher, with a fourth at the center.

Below the jar, faint stars trail down toward the bright lone star Fomalhaut (in Piscis
Austrinus) — these are the falling waters being poured out.
