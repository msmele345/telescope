## The Charioteer

Auriga is a bright pentagon of stars high overhead on winter evenings, anchored by the
golden star Capella. The charioteer carries a goat and her kids on his shoulder, and his
pentagon is strung with three of the finest open clusters in the sky.

## Mythology

The Greeks usually identified Auriga with Erichthonius, an early king of Athens who was
raised by the goddess Athena and credited with inventing the four-horse chariot. Zeus was
said to be so impressed by the invention that he placed its maker among the stars. Other
tellings make the charioteer Myrtilus, the driver who was betrayed and drowned by Pelops
after a rigged chariot race, and placed in the sky by his grieving father Hermes.

Older than either story is the goat. Capella means "little she-goat" in Latin, and the
star was linked to Amalthea, the goat who suckled the infant Zeus in a cave on Crete. The
small triangle of fainter stars beside Capella is still known as the Kids — Zeus later
made one of Amalthea's horns into the cornucopia, the horn of plenty.

## Notable stars and objects

- **Capella** — the sixth-brightest star in the night sky at magnitude 0.08 and only 42
  light-years away. It looks warm yellow because its surface is near 5500 K, much like our
  Sun's; in fact it is a pair of large yellow giants orbiting closely together.
- **Menkalinan** — "the shoulder of the rein-holder," a hot blue-white star about 82
  light-years off, and itself an eclipsing double.
- **Haldus** (Epsilon Aurigae) — one of the strangest stars known. Lying roughly 2,000
  light-years away, it normally shines near magnitude 3 but dims for nearly two years at a
  time, once every 27 years, apparently eclipsed by a vast disc of dust.
- **Haedus and Hoedus II** — the Kids, two of the goat-stars beside Capella. Haedus is a
  cool orange star; Hoedus II is fiercely hot, with a surface near 24,000 K.
- **M36, M37 and M38** — three open clusters strung along the Milky Way inside the
  pentagon, all around magnitude 6 to 7. Binoculars show them as misty patches; a small
  telescope resolves them into swarms of young stars. M37 is the richest of the three.

## How to find it

From the Northern Hemisphere, look high in the south on a February evening — Auriga passes
nearly overhead. Find Orion first, then follow his belt up and to the right past orange
Aldebaran in Taurus; the next bright star you meet is Capella.

Capella sits at one corner of Auriga's lopsided pentagon, with the little triangle of the
Kids immediately beside it. Sweep the inside of the pentagon with binoculars on a dark
night and the three Messier clusters will drift past.
