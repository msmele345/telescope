## The Chisel

Caelum is one of the faintest constellations in the sky — a thin scatter of stars between
Columba and Eridanus, representing an engraver's chisel. Nothing in it is brighter than
fourth magnitude, so it takes a dark night to see it at all.

## History

Caelum has no myth. It was invented in the 1750s by the French astronomer Nicolas-Louis
de Lacaille, who spent two years at the Cape of Good Hope cataloguing the southern sky and
filled its empty spaces with fourteen new constellations.

Where older star-makers reached for gods and heroes, Lacaille named his figures after the
instruments of the Enlightenment: a telescope, a microscope, an air pump, a painter's
easel — and here, the burin, the engraver's chisel. He first drew it as a pair of engraving
tools tied together with a ribbon, and the name was later shortened to plain Caelum, "the
chisel." It is a small, dim monument to the workshop rather than the temple.

## Notable stars

- **Alpha Caeli** — the brightest star of the constellation at magnitude 4.5, and a near
  neighbour of ours at about 66 light-years. It is a white star slightly hotter than the
  Sun, with a faint red dwarf companion.
- **Gamma¹ Caeli** — an orange giant of magnitude 4.6, some 185 light-years away, and the
  only star here with a warm tint to it.
- **Beta Caeli** — a white star of magnitude 5.1 at about 90 light-years.
- **Delta Caeli** — faint at magnitude 5.1, but only because of its distance. At roughly
  711 light-years it is a fiercely hot blue star, with a surface temperature far above any
  other star in this group.

## How to find it

Caelum sits low for most northern observers and rides comfortably high from the Southern
Hemisphere. Look on January evenings, in the stretch of sky south and west of Orion.

The reliable route is through brighter neighbours: find Rigel, Orion's blue-white foot,
then drop south through Lepus, the hare, to Columba, the dove. Caelum lies just west of
Columba, a short crooked line of faint stars between the dove and the river Eridanus. Go
out on a moonless night, away from town lights, and give your eyes time to adjust — this
is a constellation that rewards patience rather than pointing.
