## The Giraffe

Camelopardalis is a large, dim sprawl of sky between the Big Dipper, Perseus and the pole
star. It covers a great deal of ground and contains not a single star brighter than fourth
magnitude — a constellation you find by noticing an empty place and then looking harder.

## History

The name is not a camel and not a leopard but a giraffe: the Greeks called the animal
*kamelopardalis*, "camel-leopard," for its long neck and spotted hide. The constellation is
a modern invention. The Dutch astronomer and mapmaker Petrus Plancius placed it on a globe
in 1612 to fill the blank region between Ursa Major, Perseus and the north celestial pole,
and the German astronomer Jakob Bartsch printed it in a star atlas in 1624, which secured
its place in the sky.

There is no classical myth attached to these stars. The ancient Greeks simply left this
patch of the heavens unnamed, because there was nothing in it bright enough to name.

## Notable stars

- **Beta Camelopardalis** — the brightest star in the constellation at magnitude 4.0, a
  yellow supergiant roughly a thousand light-years away. Through a small telescope it
  shows a faint companion.
- **Alpha Camelopardalis** — despite the "alpha," slightly fainter than Beta at magnitude
  4.3. It is a blazing blue supergiant with a surface near 33,000 K, and it only looks
  modest because it lies about 6,900 light-years from us — one of the most distant stars
  visible to the unaided eye.
- **Gamma Camelopardalis** — a white star of magnitude 4.6 about 335 light-years away,
  marking the giraffe's head region.

## How to find it

Look north on a January evening from the Northern Hemisphere, when Camelopardalis rides
high overhead. The trick is to find its neighbours first: bright yellow Capella in Auriga
sits below it, Polaris and the Little Dipper lie to one side, and the bowl of the Big
Dipper to the other.

The large, faint region enclosed by those landmarks is the giraffe. Under city lights you
will see nothing there at all; from a dark site, a scattering of fourth- and fifth-
magnitude stars slowly emerges.
