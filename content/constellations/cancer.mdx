## The Crab

Cancer is one of the faintest constellations of the zodiac — so dim that on a moonlit or
light-polluted night, it can disappear entirely. But it hides one of the loveliest objects
in the sky.

## Mythology

In Greek myth, Cancer is the giant crab sent by Hera to harass Heracles during his second
labor: the slaying of the Hydra. While Heracles fought the many-headed serpent, the crab
crept up and pinched his foot. Heracles crushed it underfoot, and Hera, in gratitude for
the crab's loyal (if futile) effort, placed it among the stars.

The constellation's faintness has been remarked on for as long as people have written
about it — even ancient observers noted that Cancer is what you see when you fail to see
anything brighter.

## Notable stars and objects

- **Tarf** — the brightest star in Cancer, an orange giant about 290 light-years away.
- **Acubens** — the constellation's "claw," a four-star system whose name comes from
  Arabic for "the pincer."
- **The Beehive Cluster** (M44, also called Praesepe) — a beautiful open cluster of about
  a thousand stars at the heart of the crab. To the naked eye it looks like a soft fuzzy
  patch; binoculars resolve it into a swarm of glittering points. The ancient Greeks
  called it the Manger, and the two stars flanking it the Donkeys.

## How to find it

Cancer sits between Gemini (to the west) and Leo (to the east). The trick is to find both
neighbors first — the bright twins of Gemini and the unmistakable backwards-question-mark
of Leo's mane — and then look in the dim region between them. The Beehive Cluster is the
landmark to look for: a small, blurry smudge that resolves into stars in binoculars.

Cancer is best viewed in late winter and spring.
