## The Hunting Dogs

Canes Venatici is a small, faint constellation squeezed between the Big Dipper and Boötes —
two hunting dogs held on a leash by the herdsman next door. It has little to offer the naked
eye, but it is one of the richest patches of sky for a small telescope.

## History

These stars carry no ancient myth. For most of recorded history they were unclaimed filler
between the tail of the Great Bear and the figure of Boötes, and Ptolemy did not list them as
a constellation of their own.

The dogs were introduced by the Polish astronomer Johannes Hevelius, who published them in
his star atlas of 1687 along with several other new figures. The idea was tidy rather than
mythological: Boötes was already being described as a herdsman or hunter driving the bear
around the pole, so Hevelius gave him a pair of dogs to go with the job. The name stuck, and
the constellation was kept when the modern boundaries were drawn.

## Notable stars and objects

- **Chara** (Alpha² Canum Venaticorum) — the constellation's brightest star at magnitude 2.9
  and about 110 light-years away, a hot blue-white star with an unusually strong magnetic
  field that makes its brightness vary slightly as it rotates.
- **Chara** (Beta Canum Venaticorum) — a Sun-like star only 27 light-years away, and one of
  the nearer naked-eye stars to our own; magnitude 4.3.
- **M3** — a magnificent globular cluster of hundreds of thousands of ancient stars, at
  magnitude 6.2 just within reach of binoculars as a small fuzzy ball.
- **M51, the Whirlpool Galaxy** — a face-on spiral galaxy of magnitude 8.4, the first object
  in which astronomers recognised spiral structure. A small telescope shows two soft patches
  of light; a larger one begins to show the arms.
- **M63, the Sunflower Galaxy** — another spiral, magnitude 8.6, with mottled, patchy arms
  that give it its flowery name.

## How to find it

Look high in the south on a spring evening from the Northern Hemisphere, around May. Find the
Big Dipper, then drop below the curve of its handle: the sparse stars of the hunting dogs lie
in the gap between the handle and the bright orange Arcturus in Boötes.

Chara, the brighter of the two, sits closest to Arcturus and is the easiest way to anchor the
pattern. From the last star in the Dipper's handle, sweep a few degrees south-west with
binoculars to look for the Whirlpool Galaxy.
