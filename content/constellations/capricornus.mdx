## The Sea-Goat

Capricornus is one of the strangest figures in the zodiac: a creature with the front half
of a goat and the tail of a fish. It is also one of the faintest, with no truly bright
stars, but its outline of a sagging triangle is recognizable once you know where to look.

## Mythology

The sea-goat is an ancient figure, far older than the Greeks. In Sumerian and Babylonian
astronomy, the constellation was associated with the god Ea (or Enki), lord of wisdom
and the watery deep. The Greeks inherited the figure and reinterpreted it: Capricornus
is sometimes Pan, the goat-footed god, who while fleeing from the monster Typhon dove
into the Nile and transformed his lower half into a fish to escape.

The constellation marked the winter solstice in antiquity, the southernmost point of the
Sun's path. We still use this fact: the Tropic of Capricorn, the latitude on Earth where
the Sun stands directly overhead at the December solstice, is named for this constellation.
(Like Libra's, the solstice has since precessed out of Capricornus into Sagittarius — but
the name has stuck.)

## Notable stars

- **Algedi** — "the goat," a wide naked-eye double star at the head of the sea-goat. Even
  from a city, you can see two stars where there appears to be one.
- **Dabih** — "the slaughterer," another naked-eye double, this one a bit fainter and
  harder to split.
- **Deneb Algedi** — "the goat's tail," the brightest star in the constellation, a white
  giant about 39 light-years away.

## How to find it

Capricornus is best viewed in late summer and autumn, low in the southern sky. Look east
of Sagittarius. The constellation forms a wide triangle that sags in the middle — it has
been likened to a tilted boat, or a faint sagging smile. None of the stars are bright,
so dark skies help.
