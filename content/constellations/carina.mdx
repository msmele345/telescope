## The Keel

Carina is the keel of the Argo — the ship that carried Jason and his crew to the edge of the
known world. It rides low and bright through the far southern sky, and it holds Canopus, the
second-brightest star in all the heavens.

## Mythology

The Argo was built for Jason's voyage to Colchis in search of the Golden Fleece. Aboard it
sailed the greatest heroes of the age — Heracles, Orpheus, Castor and Pollux — and its prow
was said to be cut from an oak of Dodona that could speak and give counsel. When the voyage
was done, Jason dedicated the ship to Poseidon, and the gods set it among the stars.

For centuries the whole ship sailed the sky as one vast constellation, Argo Navis. It was so
large and unwieldy that astronomers eventually broke it into pieces: the keel became Carina,
the stern Puppis, and the sails Vela. The three still sit side by side, and with a little
imagination you can reassemble the ship from them.

## Notable stars

- **Canopus** — the second-brightest star in the night sky at magnitude −0.72, a white giant
  about 313 light-years away. Spacecraft have long used it as a navigation reference, because
  it is brilliant, isolated, and far from the plane of the planets.
- **Miaplacidus** — the closest of Carina's bright stars at about 111 light-years, hot and
  blue-white, shining at magnitude 1.68.
- **Avior** — an orange giant some 630 light-years off, cooler than its neighbours and
  noticeably warmer in tint.
- **Aspidiske** — a white supergiant at magnitude 2.25, nearly 700 light-years away. Its name
  comes from a Greek word for a small shield.
- **Theta Carinae** — the leading star of a loose scattering of blue stars, and one of the
  hottest stars easily seen without a telescope, with a surface near 30,000 K.

## How to find it

Carina belongs to the Southern Hemisphere, where it stands high on autumn evenings around
March. From far southern latitudes it is overhead; from the northern tropics only parts of it
clear the horizon, and Canopus is the one star most northern observers ever glimpse.

Start with Sirius and follow the sky almost due south: the next truly brilliant star you
meet is Canopus. From there, Avior and Aspidiske form two corners of the False Cross, an
X-shaped pattern Carina shares with Vela — often mistaken for the Southern Cross, which lies
further east and is smaller and brighter.
