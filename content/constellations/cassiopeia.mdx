## The Queen

Cassiopeia is a crooked W of five bright stars, sitting opposite the Big Dipper on the far
side of Polaris. It is the vain queen of Greek myth, bound to her throne and wheeling
upside down around the pole for half of every night.

## Mythology

Cassiopeia was queen of Aethiopia and wife of King Cepheus, who stands beside her in the
sky. She boasted that she — or in other tellings her daughter Andromeda — was more
beautiful than the Nereids, the sea nymphs. Poseidon took the insult personally and sent
the sea monster Cetus to ravage the coast.

An oracle told Cepheus the only remedy was to sacrifice Andromeda, so the girl was chained
to a rock at the water's edge. The hero Perseus, flying home from killing Medusa, saw her,
destroyed the monster, and married her. The whole cast was eventually set among the stars:
Cassiopeia, Cepheus, Andromeda, Perseus and Cetus all neighbour one another. As a final
punishment the queen was fixed to her chair so that she spends part of each night hanging
head downward.

## Notable stars

- **Schedar** — the brightest star of the W, a yellow-orange giant about 229 light-years
  away. Its name comes from the Arabic for "the breast."
- **Caph** — the nearest of the bright five at about 55 light-years, and a white star that
  spins so fast it is visibly flattened.
- **Navi** — a fierce blue star of around 30,000 K that varies unpredictably in
  brightness; it sits at the centre point of the W. Its odd name was coined in the 1960s
  by the astronaut Gus Grissom.
- **Ruchbah** — "the knee," a white star about 99 light-years off.
- **Achird** — faint at magnitude 3.4 but remarkably close, only 19 light-years away. A
  small telescope splits it into a yellow sun-like star with an orange companion.

## How to find it

Cassiopeia is circumpolar from most of the Northern Hemisphere, so it never sets — but it
rides highest on November evenings, nearly overhead. Look for the W (or M, depending on
the hour) of five stars of roughly equal brightness.

The quickest route is through Polaris: Cassiopeia and the Big Dipper sit on opposite sides
of the pole, so when one is high the other is low. From the W, the hazy smudge of the
Andromeda Galaxy lies a short hop to the south.
