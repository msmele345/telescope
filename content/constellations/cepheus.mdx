## The King

Cepheus is a tall house-shaped figure of modest stars wedged between Cassiopeia and the
pole. It never sets from most of the Northern Hemisphere, and though none of its stars are
dazzling, it holds one of the most important variable stars in astronomy.

## Mythology

Cepheus was the king of Aethiopia, husband of the vain queen Cassiopeia and father of
Andromeda. When Cassiopeia boasted that her daughter was more beautiful than the Nereids,
Poseidon sent the sea monster Cetus to ravage the coast. An oracle told Cepheus that the
only way to save his kingdom was to chain Andromeda to a rock at the water's edge as an
offering — and the unhappy king did it.

Perseus arrived in time, killed the monster, and married Andromeda. The whole cast was
later set among the stars: Cepheus and Cassiopeia on their thrones, Andromeda beside them,
Perseus nearby, and Cetus banished to the waters far to the south.

## Notable stars

- **Alderamin** — the brightest star of the constellation at magnitude 2.4, about 49
  light-years away. It spins so fast that it bulges at the equator, and thanks to the
  slow wobble of Earth's axis it will serve as the pole star in a few thousand years.
- **Alfirk** — a hot blue giant some 595 light-years off, and the prototype of the Beta
  Cephei variables, stars that pulse gently over a matter of hours.
- **Delta Cephei** — the most famous star here. Its brightness rises and falls on a
  regular five-day cycle, and that regularity let astronomers work out the distances to
  other galaxies. It lies almost a thousand light-years away.
- **The Garnet Star** — Mu Cephei, a deep red supergiant and one of the largest stars
  known, glowing at magnitude 4.1 from over 5,000 light-years away. Binoculars bring out
  its startling colour.
- **Errai** — the star at the peak of the "roof," a nearby orange star about 45
  light-years away with a known planet in orbit.

## How to find it

Look north on an October evening. Find the W of Cassiopeia, then look just beyond it,
towards Cygnus — Cepheus sits right beside it, drawn as a simple house: a square of four
stars topped by a pointed roof. Errai marks the peak, Alderamin one corner of the base.

From northern latitudes the constellation circles the pole all year, so it is always
somewhere above the horizon; autumn evenings simply put it highest.
