## The Sea Monster

Cetus is one of the largest constellations in the sky, a sprawling figure of faint stars
low in the autumn evenings. It has no dazzling members, but it holds one of the most famous
variable stars in the heavens and one of the Sun's nearest neighbours.

## Mythology

Cetus is the sea monster from the story of Perseus and Andromeda. Queen Cassiopeia boasted
that she — or her daughter Andromeda — was more beautiful than the sea nymphs, and Poseidon
answered the insult by sending a monster to ravage the coast of Aethiopia. To appease it,
King Cepheus chained Andromeda to a rock at the water's edge.

Perseus, flying home from killing Medusa, saw the princess and the beast rising from the
waves, and slew it. All the players in the drama were set in the sky together: Cepheus and
Cassiopeia, Andromeda, Perseus, and the monster itself, left below them on the far side of
the water. Greek writers describe Cetus vaguely — part whale, part serpent, part fish —
and it is still drawn today as a great lumbering creature of no particular species.

## Notable stars

- **Diphda** — the brightest star in Cetus at magnitude 2.04, about 96 light-years away.
  Its name comes from an Arabic phrase meaning "the second frog."
- **Menkar** — the nose of the monster, a cool orange giant about 220 light-years from us.
- **Mira** — "the wonderful," the first variable star ever recognized as such. This red
  giant some 419 light-years away swells and dims over about eleven months, from an easy
  naked-eye star to something only a telescope can recover.
- **Tau Ceti** — unremarkable to the eye at magnitude 3.50, but just 12 light-years away,
  making it one of the closest Sun-like stars and a perennial favourite of science fiction.
- **Kaffaljidhma** — a hot blue-white star about 82 light-years away, and a pretty double
  for small telescopes.

## How to find it

Cetus straddles the celestial equator, so it is visible from both hemispheres — look south
on November evenings from the north, or overhead and to the north from southern latitudes.

The easiest way in is from the Great Square of Pegasus: drop south from its western side
and you come to Diphda, sitting alone in an otherwise empty stretch of sky. The monster's
head, marked by Menkar, lies far to the east, below Aries and the Pleiades. Mira sits
between the two — worth checking each autumn, since it may or may not be there.
