## The Chameleon

Chamaeleon is a small, faint diamond of stars tucked close to the south celestial pole.
None of its stars is bright, but together they make a neat little lizard shape that
rewards a dark sky and a patient eye.

## History

Chamaeleon has no classical myth. It was invented at the end of the sixteenth century by
the Dutch navigators Pieter Dirkszoon Keyser and Frederick de Houtman, who catalogued the
southern stars during voyages to the East Indies — a part of the sky the Greeks and Romans
never saw. Their new constellations were engraved on a celestial globe by Petrus Plancius
in 1597 and then printed in Johann Bayer's atlas *Uranometria* in 1603, which is how they
entered the standard sky.

Most of the Keyser and de Houtman figures are exotic animals encountered on the voyages:
the bird of paradise, the flying fish, the toucan, the peacock. The chameleon belongs to
that menagerie, and it sits beside Musca, the fly — the two are often described as a
lizard forever stalking its next meal.

## Notable stars

- **Alpha Chamaeleontis** — the constellation's brightest star at magnitude 4.1, and the
  nearest of the group at about 64 light-years. It is a touch hotter and whiter than the
  Sun.
- **Gamma Chamaeleontis** — almost exactly as bright, but a cool red giant some 413
  light-years away. The colour contrast with Alpha is noticeable in binoculars.
- **Beta Chamaeleontis** — a hot blue star of magnitude 4.3, about 271 light-years off,
  with a surface temperature around 20,000 K.
- **Theta Chamaeleontis** — an orange star of magnitude 4.4 at roughly 154 light-years,
  marking the figure's trailing end.

## How to find it

Chamaeleon is circumpolar from most of the Southern Hemisphere and never rises for
northern observers. Look high in the south on an evening around April, when it stands well
clear of the horizon.

The easiest route is to find the Large Magellanic Cloud and the bright star Miaplacidus in
Carina, then look between them toward the pole. Chamaeleon's four main stars form a slim
diamond just south of Carina and Musca. All of them are fourth magnitude, so wait for your
eyes to adapt and choose a night without moonlight.
