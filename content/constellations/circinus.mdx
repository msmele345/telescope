## The Compasses

Circinus is a tiny, faint constellation tucked beside the front feet of Centaurus — a pair
of draughtsman's dividers, the kind used to scribe circles on a chart. It is little more
than one modest star with two fainter ones trailing away from it.

## History

Circinus has no myth. It was invented by the French astronomer Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille,
who spent the years 1751 and 1752 at the Cape of Good Hope cataloguing the southern sky,
and who filled its empty spaces with fourteen new constellations named after the
instruments of science and the arts. Circinus — Latin for a pair of compasses — appeared
on his chart of 1756, placed beside Norma (the set square) and Triangulum Australe, so
that the drafting tools sat together.

The ancient Greeks never named these stars, since they lie too far south to be seen from
the Mediterranean. Circinus is purely a product of the Enlightenment: a working tool,
hung in the sky by a man who measured things for a living.

## Notable stars

- **Alpha Circini** — the constellation's only reasonably bright star at magnitude 3.2,
  and a close neighbour at just 54 light-years. It is a rapidly oscillating peculiar star,
  pulsing in brightness every few minutes.
- **Beta Circini** — a white star of magnitude 4.1, about 97 light-years away, marking one
  arm of the compasses.
- **Gamma Circini** — magnitude 4.5 and far more distant at roughly 510 light-years, a
  very hot blue star and a fine double for a small telescope.

## How to find it

Circinus is a Southern Hemisphere constellation, best placed on evenings around June. The
trick is to start from Alpha Centauri, one of the brightest stars in the whole sky: Alpha
Circini sits only about four degrees to its south, close enough that the two fit in the
same binocular field.

From there the two fainter arms of the compasses stretch away to the southeast. Do not
expect much — under city skies Circinus all but vanishes. It rewards a dark night and a
little patience.
