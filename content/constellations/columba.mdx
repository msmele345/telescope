## The Dove

Columba is a small, quiet constellation of modest stars just south of the brilliant Sirius.
It is one of the newer figures in the sky — a dove, added to the gap between Canis Major
and the old ship Argo only four centuries ago.

## History

Columba was introduced by the Dutch astronomer and cartographer Petrus Plancius in 1592,
who drew it on a celestial globe using stars that had until then been left unassigned —
the scattering of faint lights south of Canis Major and Lepus. Plancius called it Columba
Noachi, "Noah's Dove," the bird sent out from the ark to look for dry land.

The placement was deliberate. Columba flies just ahead of Argo Navis, the great ship of
the southern sky, and Plancius seems to have intended the biblical ark and the classical
ship to echo one another. (Some writers later linked the dove instead to the bird that
Jason's Argonauts released to guide them through the clashing rocks, but that reading came
afterwards.) There is no ancient myth attached to these stars; Columba is a mapmaker's
invention, and a tidy one.

## Notable stars

- **Phact** — the brightest star of the dove at magnitude 2.6, a hot blue-white star about
  270 light-years away. Its name comes from an Arabic word for ring dove.
- **Wazn** — the second brightest, magnitude 3.1 and the nearest of the group at roughly 86
  light-years. It is a cooler, yellow-orange star, a noticeable contrast to Phact if you
  look at the two in turn.
- **Delta Columbae** — magnitude 3.9, about 237 light-years away, marking the eastern end
  of the figure.
- **Gamma Columbae** — fainter at magnitude 4.4, but one of the hottest stars here and the
  most distant of the main pattern at some 850 light-years.
- **Al Kurud** — magnitude 5.0 and right at the edge of easy naked-eye visibility. The name
  means "the apes," a leftover from an older Arabic grouping of these southern stars.

## How to find it

Columba is best seen on January evenings, and it climbs much higher for observers in the
Southern Hemisphere; from mid-northern latitudes it skims low along the southern horizon.

Start from Sirius, the brightest star in the sky, and look below and to the right of it —
beneath the faint box of Lepus the Hare — for a loose line of third- and fourth-magnitude stars running east to
west. Phact and Wazn sit close together near the middle, and once you have those two the
rest of the dove follows. A dark southern horizon helps; from a hazy one, Columba vanishes.
