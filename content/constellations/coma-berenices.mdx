## Berenice's Hair

Coma Berenices is a quiet scatter of faint stars between Leo and Boötes, named for the
shorn hair of an Egyptian queen. It has no star brighter than fourth magnitude, but it
holds one of the richest patches of galaxies in the sky.

## History

The name comes from a real woman. Berenice II was queen of Egypt in the third century BCE,
and when her husband Ptolemy III went to war in Syria she vowed to cut off her famous long
hair and offer it at a temple if he returned safely. He did, she kept her promise — and
the hair promptly vanished from the temple. The court astronomer Conon of Samos smoothed
over the scandal by announcing that the gods had been so pleased with the offering that
they had placed it among the stars.

For most of the following two thousand years these stars were treated as part of Leo,
usually as the tuft at the end of the lion's tail. Tycho Brahe listed Coma Berenices as a
constellation in its own right in his star catalogue at the end of the sixteenth century,
and it has stayed on the maps ever since. It is one of the few constellations named after
a historical person rather than a god or a beast.

## Notable stars and objects

- **Beta Comae Berenices** — the brightest star in the constellation at magnitude 4.3, and
  a near neighbour at just 30 light-years. It is a yellow star very similar to our own Sun
  in size, temperature and colour.
- **Gamma Comae Berenices** — a cooler orange star of magnitude 4.4, about 170 light-years
  away, marking the northern corner of the figure.
- **Diadem** — the "crown jewel" at the southern end, magnitude 5.2 and under 60
  light-years away. It is a close pair of near-identical stars, too tight to split without
  a good telescope.
- **M64, the Black Eye Galaxy** — a spiral galaxy of magnitude 8.5, named for the dark
  band of dust that sweeps across one side of its bright core. A small telescope shows a
  hazy oval; a larger one begins to show the "eye."

## How to find it

Look high in the south on a May evening from the Northern Hemisphere, in the empty-looking
gap between Leo's tail star Denebola and the bright orange Arcturus in Boötes. The
constellation is a loose L of faint stars — easiest to confirm from a dark site, where a
soft sprinkle of stars appears just south of Gamma Comae Berenices.

Sweep the area with binoculars even if you cannot trace the figure. This is the direction
of the vast Coma and Virgo galaxy clusters, and the faint smudges you catch there are
galaxies tens to hundreds of millions of light-years away.
