## The Southern Crown

Corona Australis is a small, quiet arc of modest stars tucked beneath the Archer's feet.
None of its stars is bright, but together they trace a delicate half-circle that is
unmistakable once you have found it — a crown laid down in the southern sky.

## Mythology

The Greeks saw this curve of stars as a wreath or crown, and most often placed it at the
feet of Sagittarius, the Archer. It is the southern counterpart of Corona Borealis, the
crown Dionysus set among the stars for Ariadne, and the two were sometimes told as a
matched pair.

Ancient writers did not agree on whose wreath it was. Whatever the story, the figure was
known to the Greeks early: Ptolemy listed it among his forty-eight constellations in the
second century.

## Notable stars

- **Alfecca Meridiana** — Alpha Coronae Australis, the crown's brightest star at magnitude
  4.1, a hot white star about 130 light-years away. Its name echoes that of Alphecca in
  the northern crown, with "Meridiana" marking it as the southern one.
- **Beta Coronae Australis** — equal in brightness to Alfecca Meridiana at magnitude 4.1,
  but a yellow giant lying far beyond it, some 508 light-years away.
- **Gamma Coronae Australis** — a close pair of near-twin yellow-white stars about 60
  light-years off, each near magnitude 5, orbiting one another every 120 years or so.

## How to find it

Look south on an August evening; southern observers will find it riding high, while from
mid-northern latitudes it skims the horizon and is easily lost in haze.

Start from Sagittarius and its Teapot asterism. Corona Australis lies just below the
Teapot's base, a shallow arc of faint stars curving away to the southeast. A dark sky
helps — nothing here is brighter than fourth magnitude — but the shape is so neat that
once seen it is hard to unsee.
