## The Northern Crown

Corona Borealis is a small, quiet constellation — a neat arc of seven stars curving like a
tiara between Boötes and Hercules. It has no bright showpieces and no deep-sky wonders, but
once you have found the little horseshoe of stars you never lose it again.

## Mythology

The Greeks saw here the crown of Ariadne, daughter of King Minos of Crete. She gave Theseus
the thread that led him out of the Labyrinth after he killed the Minotaur, then sailed away
with him — only to be abandoned on the island of Naxos while she slept.

There the god Dionysus found her, and married her. The crown he gave her at the wedding,
worked in gold and jewels, he afterwards threw up into the sky so that their marriage would
be remembered, and its gems became the stars of the arc we see today. Other tellings make
the crown a gift from Aphrodite. Arab astronomers, meanwhile, saw the same curve of stars as
a broken dish or a beggar's bowl.

## Notable stars

- **Alphecca** — the jewel of the crown, and the only star in it brighter than third
  magnitude, at 2.2. A hot white star about 75 light-years away, sometimes also called
  Gemma, "the gem."
- **Nusakan** — the second brightest, magnitude 3.7, about 114 light-years off. It sits
  one step along the arc from Alphecca, toward Boötes.
- **Gamma Coronae Borealis** — magnitude 3.8 and around 145 light-years away, a hot
  blue-white star on the far side of Alphecca from Nusakan.

## How to find it

Look high in the sky on a July evening from the Northern Hemisphere. Start with
Arcturus, the brilliant orange star in Boötes, and move east — a hand's width or so — until
you meet a small arc of faint stars, with Alphecca the brightest, near the bottom of the curve. The
huge, dimmer figure of Hercules lies just beyond it on the other side.

The crown is faint enough that city lights will hide all but Alphecca. From a dark site the
whole arc appears at once, and it is unmistakable: nothing else in that part of the sky is
shaped like it.
