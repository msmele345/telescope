## The Crow

Corvus is a small, compact constellation whose four brightest stars form a tidy lopsided
quadrilateral — sometimes called the Sail or Spica's Spanker. It is faint company for its
brilliant neighbour Spica, but the little box of stars is surprisingly easy to pick out.

## Mythology

In Greek myth the crow was the servant of Apollo, sent with a cup to fetch water from a
spring. The bird dawdled, waiting for figs on a nearby tree to ripen, and returned long
overdue — clutching a water snake in its claws and blaming the serpent for the delay.
Apollo saw through the lie at once and flung all three into the sky: the crow, the cup
(Crater), and the snake (Hydra). The crow was set just out of reach of the cup, condemned
to eternal thirst.

An older version explains the crow's colour. The bird, then white and able to speak,
carried Apollo news that his mortal love Coronis had been unfaithful. For bringing such a
message the god scorched its feathers black, and crows have been black ever since.

## Notable stars

- **Gienah** — the brightest star of the crow, at magnitude 2.59 and about 165 light-years
  away. Its name comes from the Arabic for "the wing," and its surface burns at roughly
  14,000 K, giving it a hot blue-white glow.
- **Kraz** — magnitude 2.65 and around 140 light-years off, a yellow star of about 5,500 K,
  much like a swollen, more luminous version of our Sun.
- **Algorab** — magnitude 2.95 at some 88 light-years, another blue-white star and the
  closest of the quadrilateral's four corners.
- **Minkar** — magnitude 3.00 but far more distant, about 303 light-years away, and cool
  enough at 4,700 K to show a faint orange tint.
- **Alchiba** — despite carrying the alpha designation, only magnitude 4.02. It sits just
  48 light-years away, making it the nearest of Corvus's stars.

## How to find it

Look for Corvus on evenings around May, low in the south from the Northern Hemisphere and
high overhead for observers further south, who get much the better view. The simplest
route is to follow the arc of the Big Dipper's handle to Arcturus, then on to Spica — Corvus
sits just below and to the right of Spica, a small, neat box tilted on its side.

The long body of Hydra runs beneath it, and the faint cup of Crater lies just to the west:
crow, cup and serpent, still arranged exactly as Apollo left them.
