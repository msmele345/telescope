## The Cup

Crater is a small, faint pattern of modest stars south of Leo — a goblet tipped on its side
in the spring sky. Nothing in it is bright, but its shape is a tidy little bowl once your
eyes adjust, and it comes with one of the better myths in the sky.

## Mythology

Crater is the cup of Apollo, and its story belongs to its neighbour Corvus the crow. Apollo
sent the crow to fetch water in this cup. On the way the crow found a fig tree with unripe
fruit and waited several days for the figs to ripen, forgetting his errand entirely.

Returning late, the crow seized a water snake and invented an excuse: the snake, he said,
had blocked the spring. Apollo saw through the lie at once and flung all three into the
sky — the crow, the cup, and the water snake Hydra. There the crow sits
forever beside the cup of water he cannot reach, with Hydra coiled below to keep him from
it.

## Notable stars

- **Delta Crateris** — the constellation's brightest star at magnitude 3.6, a yellowish
  giant about 195 light-years away.
- **Alkes** — its Arabic name means "the cup." At magnitude 4.1 it lies about 174
  light-years off.
- **Gamma Crateris** — equal in brightness to Alkes but much nearer, around 84
  light-years, and noticeably hotter and whiter.
- **Beta Crateris** — a hot white star of magnitude 4.5, some 266 light-years away,
  sitting at the foot of the goblet.

## How to find it

Crater is best seen on April evenings, and it sits high and comfortable for Southern
Hemisphere observers while northern ones must look low toward the south.

Start from Leo and drop south from the lion's hindquarters, well below the line of Denebola
and Zosma. Crater is the faint cup directly beneath Leo, riding on the long body of Hydra.
Corvus, the lopsided quadrilateral of the crow, lies just to the east of it (to its left, for
northern observers facing south), and is easier to spot — find it first, then step west.
Under city lights the cup may vanish entirely; give it a dark sky and let your eyes settle.
