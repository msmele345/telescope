## The Southern Cross

Crux is the smallest constellation in the sky and one of the most famous — four bright
stars in a compact cross, hanging in the far southern sky. For travellers below the
equator it does the job Polaris does in the north: it points the way to the pole.

## History

Crux has no classical myth, because the Greeks and Romans never treated it as a
constellation of its own. Its stars were visible from the Mediterranean in antiquity, but
they were counted as part of the hind legs of Centaurus, and the slow drift of precession
gradually carried them below the horizon of Europe altogether.

The cross as a separate figure came from the age of ocean voyages. European navigators
sailing south in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, among them the Italians Amerigo
Vespucci and Andrea Corsali, noted the pattern and read it as a Christian cross, a welcome
sign in unfamiliar skies. The Dutch cartographer Petrus Plancius drew it as its own figure
in the 1590s and placed it correctly on the globes he made with Jodocus Hondius in 1598
and 1600; Frederick de Houtman catalogued its stars separately from Centaurus in 1603, and
atlases such as Jakob Bartsch's in 1624 adopted it. Southern peoples had their own far older names for the same stars, and
the cross still flies today on the flags of Australia, New Zealand, Brazil, Papua New
Guinea and Samoa.

## Notable stars

- **Mimosa** — the brightest star of the cross at magnitude 1.25, a blue-white giant about
  350 light-years away with a surface near 30,000 K, hot enough to glow distinctly blue.
- **Acrux** — the foot of the cross, magnitude 1.33 and some 320 light-years off. A small
  telescope splits it into two brilliant blue stars; the companion, α² Crucis, shines at
  magnitude 1.73.
- **Gacrux** — the top of the cross, and the odd one out: a cool red giant at about 3,050 K
  and only 88 light-years away, much closer than its neighbours. A faint companion of
  magnitude 6.4 lies alongside it.
- **Delta Crucis** — the fourth and faintest arm of the cross at magnitude 2.80, another
  hot blue star about 360 light-years distant.

## How to find it

Crux is a Southern Hemisphere constellation, best seen on autumn evenings there — around
May — when it rides high in the south. From the northern tropics it barely clears the
horizon, and from mid-northern latitudes it never rises at all.

Look for the two brilliant stars of Centaurus, Alpha and Beta Centauri, and follow them
westward: they point almost directly at the cross. To find south, trace the long axis of
the cross from Gacrux through Acrux and continue about four and a half times that length —
you arrive at the empty patch of sky that marks the south celestial pole.
