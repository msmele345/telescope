## The Dolphin

Delphinus is a small constellation, but a surprisingly easy one — a tight little diamond of
stars with a tail, leaping out of the Milky Way. Once you have seen the dolphin shape, you
never lose it again.

## Mythology

In Greek myth the dolphin is a messenger of Poseidon. When the sea god wished to marry the
nereid Amphitrite, she fled from him and hid among the far islands; Poseidon sent a dolphin
to search the waters, and it found her and spoke so persuasively on his behalf that she
agreed to the marriage. In gratitude Poseidon set the dolphin's likeness among the stars.

A second story attaches to the poet Arion of Lesbos. Sailing home with a fortune won at a
music contest, he was set upon by the ship's crew, who meant to rob him and throw him
overboard. Arion asked to sing one last song; the music drew a school of dolphins alongside
the ship, and when he leapt into the sea one of them carried him safely to shore.

## Notable stars

- **Rotanev** — the brightest star of the dolphin at magnitude 3.6 and the nearest of its
  bright stars, about 97 light-years away.
- **Sualocin** — barely fainter at magnitude 3.8, a hot blue-white star some 240
  light-years off. Its odd name, with Rotanev's, is "Nicolaus Venator" spelled backwards —
  a nineteenth-century astronomer's assistant quietly smuggling himself into the sky.
- **Gamma² Delphini** — a golden star about 102 light-years away that forms a lovely
  double with its companion **Gamma¹ Delphini**, easily split in a small telescope. The
  pair marks the dolphin's nose.
- **Deneb Dulfim** — "the dolphin's tail" in Arabic, magnitude 4.0 and about 360
  light-years away.
- **Job's Coffin** — the old folk name for the compact diamond of Rotanev, Sualocin, Gamma
  and Delta Delphini that forms the dolphin's body.

## How to find it

Look for the Summer Triangle — Vega, Deneb and Altair — high overhead on September
evenings. Delphinus sits a short hop east of Altair, the Triangle's southern corner, as a
small knot of modest stars against the Milky Way.

Lying almost on the celestial equator, it is visible from both hemispheres: autumn evenings
in the north, spring evenings in the south. None of its stars is bright, so give your eyes
time to adjust and look for the shape rather than the sparkle.
