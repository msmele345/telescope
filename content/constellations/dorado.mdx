## The Dolphinfish

Dorado is a small, quiet constellation of the far southern sky, named for the fast-swimming
dolphinfish that sailors watched chasing flying fish off the bows of their ships. Its stars
are modest, but it holds one of the great sights of the southern heavens: most of the Large
Magellanic Cloud, a whole satellite galaxy of our own.

## History

Dorado has no classical myth. It first appeared on a celestial globe published in Amsterdam
in 1598 by the Dutch cartographer Petrus Plancius, drawn from observations made by the
navigators Pieter Dirkszoon Keyser and Frederick de Houtman on voyages to the East Indies.
Those two men charted the southern sky that Greek and Arab astronomers had never seen, and
the twelve constellations built from their measurements were engraved for a wide audience in
Johann Bayer's *Uranometria* of 1603.

The name has been read two ways. *Dorado* is the Spanish and Portuguese name for the
dolphinfish, or mahi-mahi — a brilliantly coloured ocean fish familiar to every sailor on
those routes. Later writers sometimes called the constellation the Swordfish or the
Goldfish, and the goldfish version still turns up in older star atlases, but the sailors'
dolphinfish is the figure the Dutch had in mind.

## Notable stars

- **Alpha Doradus** — the brightest star in the constellation at magnitude 3.3, a hot
  blue-white pair about 176 light-years away.
- **Beta Doradus** — a yellow supergiant roughly 1,040 light-years off and one of the
  brightest Cepheid variables in the sky. It swells and shrinks on a regular cycle,
  brightening and dimming by a noticeable amount over a few days; stars like it are the
  yardsticks astronomers use to measure distances to other galaxies.
- **Gamma Doradus** — a nearby yellow-white star only 66 light-years away, at magnitude 4.3.
- **Zeta Doradus** — the closest of Dorado's bright stars, a Sun-like star just 38
  light-years from us, shining at magnitude 4.7.

## How to find it

Dorado is a Southern Hemisphere constellation, best seen on January evenings high in the
south. From the Northern Hemisphere it never rises for most observers.

The easiest route is through Canopus, the brilliant star in Carina: from there, look toward
the south celestial pole and you will come to a soft, detached patch of light that looks
like a torn-off piece of the Milky Way. That is the Large Magellanic Cloud, and Dorado's
faint chain of stars runs alongside it. Binoculars turn the Cloud into a mottled glow, and
a small telescope picks out the Tarantula Nebula inside it — the most violent star-forming
region known in our corner of the universe.
