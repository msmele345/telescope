## The Dragon

Draco is a long, winding chain of modest stars that coils around the north celestial pole,
from a compact head near Hercules all the way to a tail tucked between the two Bears. It is
one of the largest constellations in the sky, and for northern observers it never sets.

## Mythology

To the Greeks, Draco was Ladon, the hundred-headed dragon set to guard the golden apples in
the garden of the Hesperides. Killing it was the eleventh labour of Heracles, and in the sky
the hero still stands over the dragon's head with his foot on its skull. Hera, who had set
Ladon to the task, placed the beast among the stars in gratitude for its long watch.

Other tellings make Draco the dragon slain by Cadmus before he founded Thebes, or one of the
serpentine Titans cast into the sky after the war against the Olympian gods. Whichever story
you prefer, the figure is always a guardian — endlessly circling the pole, never allowed to
set and rest.

## Notable stars

- **Eltanin** — the brightest star of Draco and the brighter eye of the dragon's head, a
  cool orange giant about 148 light-years away at magnitude 2.2. It passes almost directly
  overhead from London, which earned it the nickname "the zenith star."
- **Rastaban** — the other eye, its name from the Arabic for "head of the serpent." It
  looks fainter than Eltanin only because it is much further off, some 362 light-years away.
- **Thuban** — the historical star of the constellation. Around 2700 BCE precession placed
  Thuban at the north celestial pole, making it the pole star of the pyramid builders.
  Today it is a quiet magnitude 3.7 star about 309 light-years away.
- **Altais and Edasich** — two of the kinks in the dragon's long body, both around 100
  light-years from us and both easy to pick out once you have the shape in mind.
- **Alsafi** — a faint but very near neighbour, a Sun-like star just 19 light-years away.

## How to find it

Draco is best in the evenings around July, when it climbs high overhead for Northern
Hemisphere observers. Start at the Big Dipper: the dragon's tail begins between the Dipper's
bowl and Polaris, then the body sweeps away from the pole, loops around Ursa Minor, and runs
out towards Hercules.

The head is the easiest part. Look for a small lopsided quadrilateral of stars just west of
the Keystone of Hercules — Eltanin and Rastaban are the two brightest of the four. From
there, trace the faint body back towards the pole, and the whole dragon unwinds.
