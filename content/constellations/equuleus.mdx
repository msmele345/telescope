## The Little Horse

Equuleus is the second-smallest constellation in the sky, a faint little trapezium of stars
tucked beside the great square of Pegasus. Nothing in it is brighter than fourth magnitude,
but it is one of the 48 constellations catalogued by Ptolemy almost two thousand years ago.

## Mythology

The Greeks told several stories about this small horse. In one, it is Celeris — the brother
or offspring of Pegasus — given by Mercury to Castor, one of the twins of Gemini. In
another, it is the horse Neptune struck from the ground with his trident during his contest
with Athena for the city of Athens, the god's gift set against the goddess's olive tree.

Because only the head of the figure appears, the constellation is sometimes called Equus
Primus, "the First Horse" — it rises just ahead of Pegasus, so the little horse's head
clears the horizon before its famous neighbour.

## Notable stars

- **Kitalpha** — the brightest star of the group at magnitude 3.9, about 186 light-years
  away. Its name comes from an Arabic phrase meaning "a piece of the horse."
- **Delta Equulei** — magnitude 4.5 and the nearest of the group at around 60 light-years,
  a close binary pair whose two Sun-like stars orbit each other in a little over five
  years.
- **Gamma Equulei** — magnitude 4.7, about 115 light-years away, a white star whose
  brightness flickers very slightly over a matter of minutes.

## How to find it

Equuleus is visible from both hemispheres, best on September evenings — autumn in the north,
spring in the south. Start with the Great Square of Pegasus, and find Enif, the bright
orange nose of the winged horse, off the Square's western side. Equuleus lies just east of
Enif, a small faint wedge of stars roughly a fist's width away.

Be patient and find a dark sky. This is a constellation you must look for deliberately; its
stars will not catch your eye on their own.
