## The River

Eridanus is a long, winding chain of faint stars that spills south from the foot of Orion
and does not stop until it reaches the deep southern sky. It is the sixth-largest
constellation of all, and tracing its full length from end to end is one of the quiet
pleasures of a January night.

## Mythology

The Greeks knew Eridanus simply as a river — sometimes the Po, sometimes the Nile, often
no particular river at all but the great stream encircling the world. Its best-known story
belongs to Phaethon, the mortal son of Helios, who begged to drive his father's sun
chariot for a day. He could not hold the horses; the chariot scorched the Earth, and Zeus
struck him down with a thunderbolt to stop the burning.

Phaethon fell blazing out of the sky and into the river Eridanus, which quenched the fire.
His sisters, the Heliades, wept on its banks until they were turned into poplar trees, and
their tears hardened into amber. The river was set among the stars as a memorial. It
begins near Rigel, Orion's brilliant foot, and flows away toward the south.

## Notable stars

- **Achernar** — "the river's end," the ninth-brightest star in the sky at magnitude 0.46
  and about 144 light-years away. It is a furiously hot blue star, spinning so fast that
  it is flattened into a squashed sphere, one of the flattest stars known.
- **Cursa** — the star marking the river's source, just beside Rigel, at magnitude 2.79 and
  about 89 light-years away.
- **Zaurak** — a cool red giant of magnitude 2.95, noticeably orange to the eye.
- **Acamar** — once considered the river's end, before southern voyages revealed Achernar
  beyond it. A fine double star about 161 light-years away.
- **Ran** — at only 11 light-years, one of the nearest stars visible to the naked eye, and
  known to host planets of its own.
- **Keid** — a nearby triple system about 17 light-years away, containing a white dwarf
  that small telescopes can pick out.

## How to find it

Start at Rigel, the blue-white foot of Orion, and look just to its west for Cursa. From
there the river meanders west, then south, then west again in a long series of loops —
never bright, but never quite breaking.

From the Southern Hemisphere on a summer evening, the whole river is on display, ending at
Achernar high in the south. Northern observers lose the southern end: Achernar never rises
for most of Europe and North America.
