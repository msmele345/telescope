## The Furnace

Fornax is a faint, quiet constellation tucked into a bend of the river Eridanus. It has no
bright stars and no myth, but it is a good test of a dark sky — if you can trace its thin
arc of fifth-magnitude stars, your observing site is a good one.

## History

Fornax is a modern constellation. The French astronomer Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille invented
it during his expedition to the Cape of Good Hope in the early 1750s, where he catalogued
nearly ten thousand southern stars and filled the empty spaces between the older figures
with fourteen new ones.

Lacaille named his constellations not after gods and heroes but after the instruments of
the arts and sciences: a clock, a telescope, an air pump, a sculptor's studio. This one he
called Fornax Chemica, the chemist's furnace, after the small heating furnace used in a
laboratory of his day. The name was later shortened simply to Fornax. There is no myth
attached to it, and no ancient tradition — these stars were left over, and Lacaille gave
them a job.

## Notable stars

- **Fornacis** — the brightest star of the constellation at magnitude 3.9, and a close
  neighbour of ours at only 46 light-years. It is a yellow-white star somewhat hotter than
  the Sun.
- **Beta Fornacis** — a yellow giant of magnitude 4.5, about 169 light-years away.
- **Nu Fornacis** — magnitude 4.7 and some 361 light-years distant, a hot blue-white star
  many times more luminous than the Sun.

## How to find it

Fornax is best placed on December evenings, and it favours the Southern Hemisphere, where
it rides high overhead; northern observers will find it low in the south.

Start with Eridanus, the long winding river that flows away from Orion's foot toward the
southern horizon. Fornax sits inside one of the river's wide bends, with Cetus the whale
above it and brilliant Achernar, the river's end, further south. Look for a shallow arc of
three modest stars; Acamar, the bright double at Eridanus's southern edge, lies just below
and helps confirm you are in the right place.
