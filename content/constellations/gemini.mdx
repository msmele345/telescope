## The Twins

Gemini is one of the easier constellations to spot — two parallel lines of stars topped by
two bright "head" stars, Castor and Pollux, hanging side by side like a pair of twin
brothers.

## Mythology

The twins are Castor and Pollux, sons of the Spartan queen Leda. In one version of the
story, Pollux was the immortal son of Zeus, while Castor was the mortal son of Leda's
husband. The two were inseparable companions — they sailed with Jason on the Argo and
fought in the Trojan War.

When Castor was killed in battle, Pollux begged Zeus to share his immortality with his
brother so they would never be parted. Zeus agreed, and placed them both in the sky as the
constellation Gemini, where they remain together still.

In Roman tradition, the twins were guardians of sailors at sea, and St. Elmo's fire — the
glow seen on a ship's masts during electrical storms — was taken as a sign of their
presence.

## Notable stars

- **Pollux** — an orange giant about 34 light-years away, slightly the brighter of the two
  head stars. It is the closest known giant star with an exoplanet (Pollux b, discovered
  in 2006).
- **Castor** — looks like a single star to the eye, but is actually a system of six stars
  bound together gravitationally. To early telescopes it was a famous double, and one of
  the first stars proven to be a binary.

## How to find it

Find Orion, then look up and to the left (in the Northern Hemisphere). Castor and Pollux
are a bright, distinctive pair — Pollux slightly more orange and slightly brighter, Castor
more white. Once you have the heads, the bodies of the twins stretch back toward Orion's
shoulder.

Gemini is best viewed in winter and early spring, when it sits high in the southern sky.
