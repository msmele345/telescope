## The Crane

Grus is a long-necked wading bird stretched across the far southern sky, a bright figure in
a quiet corner of the heavens. Its two leading stars make a striking pair: one hot and
blue-white, the other cool and distinctly orange.

## History

Grus is not an ancient constellation and carries no Greek myth. Its stars were charted in
the 1590s by the Dutch navigators Pieter Dirkszoon Keyser and Frederick de Houtman during
voyages to the East Indies, where the southern sky stood high overhead and European maps
were simply blank. The cartographer Petrus Plancius placed their new figures on a celestial
globe in 1598, and Johann Bayer engraved them in his great star atlas *Uranometria* of
1603, which is how they entered common use.

Before that, these stars were generally reckoned part of Piscis Austrinus, the Southern
Fish. Some early charts called the new bird Phoenicopterus, the Flamingo, before Grus the
Crane settled as the accepted name. De Houtman's own list had called the figure a heron.

## Notable stars

- **Alnair** — the brightest star of the crane at magnitude 1.74, about 101 light-years
  away. Its surface burns near 16,000 K, giving it a fierce blue-white colour. The name is
  Arabic, from a phrase meaning "the bright one of the fish's tail" — a memory of the days
  when these stars belonged to Piscis Austrinus.
- **Beta Gruis** — magnitude 2.10 and about 170 light-years off, a cool red giant at
  roughly 2,750 K. Set beside Alnair, it makes one of the sky's better colour contrasts.
- **Aldhanab** — magnitude 3.01, some 203 light-years away, another hot blue-white star
  marking the crane's head.
- **The Delta Gruis pair** — Delta¹ and Delta² Gruis, magnitudes 3.97 and 4.11, sit close
  enough together to look like a wide double to the naked eye, though they are unrelated
  and lie at different distances.

## How to find it

Grus is a Southern Hemisphere constellation, best seen on October evenings — spring in the
south, autumn in the north. From southern latitudes it rides high overhead; from the mid
northern latitudes only its topmost stars clear the horizon, if at all.

The easiest route is through Fomalhaut, the lone bright star of Piscis Austrinus. Grus lies
directly below it, with Aldhanab marking the bird's head nearest Fomalhaut and the body
trailing south to Alnair and the orange glow of Beta Gruis.
