## The Pendulum Clock

Horologium is a long, faint trickle of stars in the far southern sky, named for a pendulum
clock. Nothing in it is brighter than fourth magnitude, and it takes a dark night and some
patience to trace.

## History

Horologium is a modern invention. The French astronomer Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille mapped
the southern sky from the Cape of Good Hope in 1751–52 and filled its empty spaces with
fourteen new constellations, nearly all of them instruments of science and the arts. This
one he called Horologium Oscillatorium — the pendulum clock — honouring Christiaan Huygens,
who had invented the device a century earlier and transformed timekeeping, and with it
astronomy.

The name was later shortened simply to Horologium. Like Lacaille's other creations, it has
no myth attached to it: no Greek hero, no transformed nymph, just a piece of laboratory
equipment hung among the stars by an astronomer who admired precision.

## Notable stars

- **Alpha Horologii** — the constellation's brightest star at magnitude 3.9, an orange
  giant about 117 light-years away. It sits at the clock's northern end, away from the rest
  of the figure.
- **Delta Horologii** — a white star of magnitude 4.9, roughly 175 light-years distant.
- **Beta Horologii** — magnitude 5.0 and the most remote of the group at about 314
  light-years, a hot blue-white star.
- **Mu Horologii** — magnitude 5.1, some 138 light-years away, one of the faint stars
  strung along the clock's pendulum.

## How to find it

Horologium is best seen from the Southern Hemisphere on December evenings, when it rides
high overhead. Start from brilliant Achernar at the southern end of Eridanus, the River;
Horologium lies just to its west, a thin chain of faint stars curling south between
Eridanus and the small constellation Reticulum.

Do not expect a clock. The figure is really a long line of fifth-magnitude points with
Alpha set apart at one end — more a reminder of Lacaille's enthusiasm for instruments than
a picture you can see.
