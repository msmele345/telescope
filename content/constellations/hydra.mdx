## The Water Snake

Hydra is the largest constellation in the sky, a long thin ribbon of stars winding more
than a quarter of the way around the heavens. It has only one bright star, so tracing its
full length is a patient observer's reward.

## Mythology

Hydra is the many-headed serpent of Lerna, the monster Heracles faced as the second of his
twelve labours. Each time he struck off one of its heads, two more grew in its place, and
a giant crab scuttled out of the swamp to nip at his feet while he fought. Heracles solved
the problem with help: his nephew Iolaus seared each stump with a burning brand so no new
head could sprout, and the final immortal head was buried under a great rock.

The crab was placed in the sky as Cancer, and the serpent alongside it as Hydra — both set
among the stars as a memorial to the fight. In other tellings the snake belongs to a
different story altogether: it is the water snake in the tale of the raven and the cup,
the creature the raven blamed for failing to bring Apollo his water — which is why Corvus
the Crow and Crater the Cup still ride on Hydra's back.

## Notable stars

- **Alphard** — "the solitary one," and the name is earned: this orange giant of magnitude
  2.0, about 177 light-years away, sits in an empty stretch of sky with no bright rival
  near it. It marks the serpent's heart.
- **Gamma Hydrae** — magnitude 3.0, roughly 130 light-years away, a yellow giant far down
  the snake's tail toward Libra.
- **Hydrobius** — Zeta Hydrae, magnitude 3.1, about 151 light-years off, one of the stars
  forming the small crooked group that makes Hydra's head.
- **Nu Hydrae** — magnitude 3.1 and some 139 light-years away, a yellow-orange giant in
  the middle of the long body.
- **Minchir** — "the snake's snout," a fainter star at magnitude 4.4 and about 353
  light-years distant, tucked into the head.

## How to find it

Look south on an April evening — Hydra straddles the celestial equator, so observers in
both hemispheres get a good view. Start with the head, a compact knot of modest stars just
below Cancer and due east of Procyon in Canis Minor.

From there drop south-east to Alphard, unmistakable because nothing else nearby is as
bright. Then follow the faint chain of stars eastward below Leo, past the little
constellations of Crater and Corvus riding on its back, until the tail fades out beneath
Virgo. Tracing the whole snake takes a long, slow sweep across the sky.
