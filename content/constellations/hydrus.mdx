## The Little Water Snake

Hydrus is a small, faint constellation coiled close to the south celestial pole, far enough
south that most of the world has never seen it. Its three brightest stars form a long
shallow triangle in the gap between Achernar and the Large Magellanic Cloud.

## History

Hydrus is not a classical figure and carries no myth. Its stars were charted in the 1590s
by the Dutch navigators Pieter Dirkszoon Keyser and Frederick de Houtman during voyages to
the East Indies, who mapped the southern sky that Greek and Roman astronomers had never
been able to see.

The constellation first appeared on a celestial globe published by the Dutch cartographer
Petrus Plancius in 1598, and was fixed in print when Johann Bayer included it in his
*Uranometria* atlas of 1603. The name means "the little water snake," chosen to distinguish
it from the much older and far larger Hydra of the northern sky. The two are unrelated —
one is a monster of Greek legend, the other a Renaissance bookkeeping entry filling in the
blank space around the southern pole.

## Notable stars

- **Beta Hydri** — the brightest star in the constellation at magnitude 2.8, and one of the
  closest at just 24 light-years. It is a near twin of our own Sun, slightly older and
  slightly larger, giving a preview of what the Sun will look like in a few billion years.
  It is also the nearest bright Sun-like star to the south celestial pole, which made it
  useful to navigators.
- **Head of Hydrus** — the traditional name for Alpha Hydri, magnitude 2.9 and about 71
  light-years away. It is hotter and whiter than Beta, and sits closest to Achernar.
- **Gamma Hydri** — a cool red giant of magnitude 3.2, roughly 214 light-years off. At
  around 3200 K it is the one star here with obvious colour; binoculars bring out a clear
  orange-red.

## How to find it

Hydrus is best seen from the Southern Hemisphere on December evenings, when it rides high
in the south. Northern observers below roughly 10°N may glimpse its top edge, but the
constellation never rises for most of Europe and North America.

Start from Achernar, the brilliant star at the end of Eridanus. Hydrus begins just south of
it: Alpha Hydri lies nearby, and from there the figure stretches away toward the pole, with
Beta and Gamma marking the far corners. The Small Magellanic Cloud sits on its border, a
faint patch of light that makes a better signpost than any of Hydrus's own stars.
