## The Indian

Indus is a faint, quiet constellation of the far southern sky, drawn as a man holding
spears or arrows. It has no bright stars and no deep-sky showpieces, but it does hold one
of the Sun's closest neighbours.

## History

Indus is not an ancient figure and carries no myth. It was created in the late sixteenth
century from observations made by the Dutch navigators Pieter Dirkszoon Keyser and
Frederick de Houtman during a trading voyage to the East Indies — the two men charted
the southern stars that classical astronomers had never seen. The Dutch cartographer
Petrus Plancius placed the new constellation on a celestial globe in 1598, and Johann
Bayer printed it in his great atlas *Uranometria* in 1603, which fixed it in the sky for
good.

Exactly which people the figure was meant to represent is unclear. Early depictions show
an unclothed man carrying arrows or spears, and he has been read variously as a native of
the East Indies, of Madagascar, or of the Americas — a European sailor's generic emblem
of the distant lands the voyage had reached, rather than a portrait of anyone in
particular.

## Notable stars

- **Alpha Indi** — the brightest star in the constellation at magnitude 3.1, an orange
  giant about 100 light-years away.
- **Beta Indi** — a cooler orange giant of magnitude 3.7, much more distant at roughly
  600 light-years.
- **Epsilon Indi** — unremarkable to the eye at magnitude 4.7, but it lies just 12
  light-years from Earth, making it one of the nearest stars visible without a telescope.
- **Theta Indi** — a white star of magnitude 4.4 some 97 light-years away, and a pleasant
  double in a small telescope.

## How to find it

Indus is a Southern Hemisphere constellation, best seen on September evenings high in the
south. Look for it beneath the bright pair of Grus and Pavo: if you can find Achernar and
the long neck of the crane, Indus lies west of Grus, filling the dim space between it and
Pavo's tail.

Do not expect a clear figure. Indus is a scattering of modest stars, and the reward is
knowing that one of them, faint Epsilon Indi, is practically next door.
