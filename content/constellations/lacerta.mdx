## The Lizard

Lacerta is a small, faint zigzag of stars tucked between Cygnus and Andromeda. It has no
bright star and no myth behind it — just a narrow strip of sky that had to be filled with
something.

## History

Lacerta was invented by the Polish astronomer Johannes Hevelius, who introduced it in
1687. He was in the habit of inventing constellations for the leftover gaps between the
ancient figures, and this one is a good example: a long, thin space between Cygnus,
Cepheus, Andromeda and Pegasus, too cramped for anything grand. A lizard, he decided,
would fit.

Others had tried. Augustin Royer had proposed a sceptre and hand of justice for the French
king a few years earlier, and later mapmakers attempted to rename the region for various
monarchs. None of those flattering titles survived. Hevelius's modest little reptile did,
and it remains one of the 88 official constellations today.

Because its stars form a shallow W or zigzag, Lacerta is sometimes nicknamed "Little
Cassiopeia" — a useful warning for beginners sweeping the autumn sky, since the real
Cassiopeia lies not far to the north.

## Notable stars

- **Alpha Lacertae** — the brightest star in the constellation at magnitude 3.8, a
  white star about 102 light-years away. Even at its best it is only a modest naked-eye
  object from a dark site.
- **Beta Lacertae** — a yellowish star of magnitude 4.4, roughly 170 light-years distant,
  marking the northern end of the zigzag.
- **HR 8498** — magnitude 4.1 and about 627 light-years away, cooler and distinctly more
  orange than Alpha, and the second brightest star here.

## How to find it

Look high overhead on an October evening from mid-northern latitudes. Find the Summer
Triangle's Deneb, at the tail of Cygnus, then move east toward the W of Cassiopeia;
Lacerta lies in the gap between them, a little south of the line joining the two.

You will need a dark sky. Nothing in Lacerta is bright, and in a light-polluted suburb the
lizard simply is not there. Under good conditions, the small zigzag of fourth-magnitude
stars is a quietly satisfying thing to pick out.
