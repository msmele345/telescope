## The Lion

Leo is one of the few constellations that genuinely looks like what it claims to be — a
crouching lion with a curling mane, paws stretched forward, tail flicking behind.

## Mythology

In Greek myth, Leo is the Nemean Lion, a monstrous beast with a hide so tough that no
weapon could pierce it. Heracles was tasked with killing it as the first of his Twelve
Labors. After his arrows bounced off its skin, he wrestled it bare-handed into a cave and
strangled it. He then skinned it using one of its own claws and wore the pelt — invincible
armor — for the rest of his labors.

Leo is much older than the Greeks. Babylonian, Persian, Egyptian, and Indian astronomers
all saw a lion in these stars. The constellation rises in the spring, and in ancient
Egypt its appearance coincided with the Nile's flooding — the lion in the sky heralded
the arrival of life-giving water below.

## Notable stars

- **Regulus** — the "little king," a brilliant blue-white star about 79 light-years away
  marking the lion's heart. It sits almost exactly on the ecliptic, the path of the Sun
  through the sky, which made it a star of royal omens for thousands of years.
- **Denebola** — the lion's tail, a fast-rotating white star.
- **Algieba** — a beautiful golden double star at the base of the mane, easily resolved
  in a small telescope.

## How to find it

Look for the **Sickle** — a backwards question-mark of stars with Regulus at the bottom
forming the period. That curl is the lion's mane and head. From there, the back of the
lion stretches eastward to Denebola at the tail.

Leo is high overhead in spring evenings in the Northern Hemisphere. In late spring you
will sometimes see Mars or another planet wandering through it, since the ecliptic passes
right through the constellation.
