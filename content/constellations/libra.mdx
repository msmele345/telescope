## The Scales

Libra is the only zodiac constellation that does not represent a living thing — a pair of
balance scales, hanging quietly in the southern summer sky.

## Mythology

In ancient Babylon, Libra's stars were not scales at all but the claws of the giant
scorpion Scorpius next door — and you can still see that older identification in the
names of Libra's two brightest stars, both meaning "claw" in Arabic. The Romans split
them off into a separate constellation in the first century BCE, identifying it with the
scales of the goddess Astraea (or Justitia), who weighed the souls of the dead.

The choice was practical as well as poetic. Libra contains the autumnal equinox — the
moment when day and night are perfectly balanced — so the scales fit neatly with that
moment of cosmic equilibrium. (The equinox has since precessed into Virgo, but the name
of the constellation has stayed put for over two thousand years.)

## Notable stars

- **Zubeneschamali** — "the northern claw," a curiously colored star sometimes described
  as the only star to the naked eye that looks pale green. (Most observers see it as
  white, but reports of green tinge are persistent.)
- **Zubenelgenubi** — "the southern claw," a wide double star easily split with
  binoculars.

## How to find it

Libra sits between Virgo (to the west) and Scorpius (to the east). It is best viewed in
the late spring and summer, low in the southern sky. Once you have spotted Scorpius and
the bright red Antares, look just to the right (west) for the small triangular figure of
the scales.

Libra is not a flashy constellation — but the names of its stars carry the memory of an
older sky, when these were not scales but the claws of the great scorpion next door.
