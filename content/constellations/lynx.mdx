## The Lynx

Lynx is a faint, straggling line of stars filling the gap between Ursa Major and Auriga.
It has no bright stars, no myths and no deep-sky showpieces — and that emptiness is
exactly the point of it.

## History

Lynx was invented by the Polish astronomer Johannes Hevelius, who published it in his star
atlas late in the seventeenth century. He was filling in a blank patch of northern sky that
the ancient Greeks had left unclaimed, and he chose the name as a joke at the observer's
expense: you would need the eyes of a lynx, he said, to make anything out there.

The constellation carries no classical story, because it is only a little over three
centuries old. John Flamsteed adopted it in his catalogue of 1712, and it has kept its
place on the map ever since.

## Notable stars

- **Alpha Lyncis** — the constellation's brightest star at magnitude 3.1, an orange giant
  about 222 light-years away. It sits at the southeastern end of the chain, nearest Cancer.
- **HR 3690** — the second brightest, magnitude 3.8, a hot white star some 122 light-years
  from us and the closest of Lynx's leading stars.
- **Alsciaukat** — the only star here with a proper name, from the Arabic for "the
  thorn." It is an orange giant of magnitude 4.3, lying roughly 389 light-years away.

## How to find it

Look high in the northern sky on a February evening, between the bowl of the Big Dipper and
the bright yellow Capella in Auriga. Lynx occupies the dark ground between them.

Start at Castor and Pollux in Gemini and sweep upward and to the left: the faint zigzag of
Lynx runs from there toward the Dipper's front feet. You will need a dark sky and some
patience — Hevelius meant it to be difficult.
