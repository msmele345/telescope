## The Lyre

Lyra is a small constellation with an outsized claim on the summer sky: it contains Vega,
one of the brightest stars visible from Earth. The rest of the figure is a modest little
parallelogram hanging just below it — the strings of the instrument.

## Mythology

The lyre belonged to Orpheus, the greatest musician of Greek myth, who could charm wild
animals, trees and even stones with his playing. When his wife Eurydice died of a snake
bite, Orpheus went down into the underworld and played until Hades himself relented,
agreeing to release her on one condition: that Orpheus walk ahead and not look back until
both had reached the surface. Within sight of daylight he turned, and she was gone.

Orpheus wandered inconsolable afterwards and was eventually killed — in the best-known
version, torn apart by the followers of Dionysus. The Muses gathered his remains and buried
them, and at the command of Apollo and Zeus his lyre was set among the stars. In some older
traditions the whole constellation was itself read as a swooping vulture or eagle, a memory
preserved in Vega's name, from an Arabic phrase meaning "the falling eagle."

## Notable stars and objects

- **Vega** — a brilliant blue-white star at magnitude 0.03, and one of the nearest bright
  stars to us at just 25 light-years. Its 10,000 K surface gives it a crisp blue-white
  colour. Astronomers once used Vega as the reference point for the entire
  magnitude scale.
- **Sulafat** — the southeastern corner of the parallelogram, a hot blue giant around 635
  light-years away, far more distant than Vega yet still shining at magnitude 3.2.
- **Sheliak** — the other end of the lyre's lower edge, about 880 light-years off. It is a
  pair of stars so close together that they pull each other into egg shapes and eclipse
  one another as they orbit, so its brightness dips by nearly a magnitude every 13 days.
- **The Ring Nebula (M57)** — the glowing shell of gas thrown off by a dying star, sitting
  almost exactly between Sheliak and Sulafat. At magnitude 8.8 it needs a telescope, where
  it looks like a small grey smoke ring.

## How to find it

Look high overhead on an August evening from the Northern Hemisphere. Vega is almost
impossible to miss — it will likely be the brightest star near the zenith, and it forms the
western corner of the Summer Triangle with Deneb in Cygnus and Altair in Aquila.

Once you have Vega, look just below and to the southeast for four fainter stars in a small
slanted rectangle. That parallelogram is the body of the lyre, and the Ring Nebula hides
along its lower edge.
