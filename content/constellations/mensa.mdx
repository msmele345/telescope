## The Table

Mensa is the faintest constellation in the sky — not one of its stars reaches fourth
magnitude. It represents Table Mountain, the flat-topped peak that overlooks Cape Town,
and it is the only constellation named for a real place on Earth.

## History

Mensa was invented by the French astronomer Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille, who spent the years
1751 and 1752 at the Cape of Good Hope cataloguing the southern stars. He filled the blank
spaces of that sky with instruments of science and art — a telescope, a clock, a painter's
easel — and reserved one small patch for the mountain under which he had done the work.
He called it Mons Mensae, "the Table Mountain"; later astronomers shortened it to Mensa.

Lacaille's choice was more than sentiment. The Large Magellanic Cloud lies along the edge
of the constellation, and he recalled that the Magellanic Clouds were sometimes called the
Cape clouds, while Table Mountain was often covered in cloud when a stormy southeasterly
blew. So he set his "table" in the sky beneath the Cloud. There is no classical myth
attached to Mensa, and no older tradition — these stars were simply too far south for the
Greeks or Babylonians ever to see.

## Notable stars

- **Alpha Mensae** — the brightest star in the constellation at magnitude 5.1, and even so
  barely visible to the unaided eye. It is a yellow dwarf much like our own Sun, and a
  close neighbour at about 33 light-years.
- **Gamma Mensae** — a cooler orange star of magnitude 5.2, around 100 light-years away.
- **Beta Mensae** — magnitude 5.3, but far more distant at roughly 640 light-years, which
  makes it a genuinely luminous star rather than a nearby faint one.
- **Theta Mensae** — a hot blue-white star of magnitude 5.5, some 356 light-years off.

## How to find it

Mensa is for Southern Hemisphere observers, and really only for those under dark skies. Look
high in the south on a January evening. The signpost is the Large Magellanic Cloud, the
hazy patch of a companion galaxy that straddles Dorado and Mensa; Mensa's dim stars lie
just south of it, between the Cloud and the south celestial pole.

Do not expect a figure. Mensa is a quiet rectangle of faint stars that rewards patience and
a moonless night — a modest monument to the mountain where the southern sky was first
charted.
