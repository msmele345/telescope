## The Microscope

Microscopium is one of the faintest constellations in the sky — a small, dim rectangle of
stars south of Capricornus with not a single star brighter than fourth magnitude. It takes
a dark night and some patience to see it at all.

## History

Microscopium has no mythology. It was invented in the 1750s by the French astronomer
Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille, who spent two years at the Cape of Good Hope cataloguing the
southern sky and filled its empty spaces with fourteen new constellations named after the
instruments of science and the arts: the air pump, the chemical furnace, the sculptor's
studio, the telescope — and the compound microscope.

The region had simply gone unnamed by the Greeks, who saw little worth grouping in these
faint stars. Lacaille's instrument figures were adopted quickly and remain official today,
so a device from an eighteenth-century laboratory now sits permanently among the
constellations.

## Notable stars

- **Gamma Microscopii** — the brightest star in the constellation at magnitude 4.7, a
  yellow giant about 224 light-years away. It is thought to have passed close to our solar
  system a few million years ago.
- **Epsilon Microscopii** — very nearly as bright, a hot white star some 165 light-years
  distant.
- **Alpha Microscopii** — despite carrying the constellation's first letter, only its
  fourth-brightest star, a yellow giant around 380 light-years away.
- **Eta Microscopii** — faint at magnitude 5.5, but the most distant of the group at
  roughly 930 light-years, its light cool and orange.

## How to find it

Microscopium is best seen on September evenings, and favours observers in the Southern
Hemisphere, where it climbs high overhead. Northern viewers can catch it low in the south.

Start from Fomalhaut, the lone bright star of the autumn sky, and look west toward the
triangle of Capricornus. Microscopium lies in the gap below Capricornus and above the
tail of Sagittarius — a quiet patch where, under city lights, you may see nothing at all.
Wait for a moonless night far from town, and a faint little quadrilateral emerges.
