## The Unicorn

Monoceros is a large but faint constellation wedged between Orion and the bright stars of
the winter sky. It has no star much brighter than fourth magnitude, so it takes a dark night
to trace the unicorn at all — but the empty-looking ground it covers is rich in star clusters
and nebulae for binoculars.

## History

Monoceros is a modern invention with no classical myth behind it. The unicorn first appears
on a celestial globe made by the Dutch cartographer Petrus Plancius around 1613, filling a
stretch of dim sky east of Orion that the Greeks had left unnamed. Plancius drew several
such figures from biblical and natural-history sources, and the "one-horned" beast of
medieval bestiaries suited the gap.

Later astronomers kept it. Jakob Bartsch listed it in 1624, and Johannes Hevelius included
it in his influential star atlas of 1690, which secured its place in the sky. When the
International Astronomical Union fixed the modern list of 88 constellations in the 1920s,
Monoceros was among them.

## Notable stars

- **Alpha Monocerotis** — the brightest single star in the constellation at magnitude 3.9,
  a yellowish giant about 144 light-years away.
- **Gamma Monocerotis** — almost exactly as bright, magnitude 4.0, but far more distant at
  around 645 light-years, which tells you it is a genuinely luminous orange giant.
- **Beta Monocerotis** — the finest sight in Monoceros. A small telescope splits it into
  three blue-white stars in a neat curve; William Herschel called it one of the most
  beautiful sights in the heavens. The three stars shine together a little brighter than
  Alpha, and the brightest of them has a searing 24,000 K surface, making it the hottest of
  the constellation's bright stars, about 690 light-years off.
- **Delta Monocerotis** — a white star of magnitude 4.2, some 375 light-years away.
- **Epsilon Monocerotis** — magnitude 4.4 and the nearest of the group at about 129
  light-years; another double for small telescopes.

## How to find it

Monoceros straddles the celestial equator, so it is visible from both hemispheres. Look for
it on February evenings, high up in the north's winter sky and in the south's summer sky.

Start from Orion's belt and Betelgeuse, then look east — the region between Orion, Sirius
in Canis Major, and Procyon in Canis Minor is Monoceros. Its stars are faint enough that
the trick is to find the bright borders first and accept that the unicorn lives in the
quiet space inside them.
