## The Fly

Musca is a small, neat constellation tucked just south of the Southern Cross — the only
insect among the 88 constellations. It is faint but compact, and from southern latitudes it
never sets.

## History

Musca has no myth. Its stars were too far south for the Greeks to see, and they were first
charted by European navigators sailing the southern oceans — the Dutch pilots Pieter
Dirkszoon Keyser and Frederick de Houtman, who recorded positions of southern stars in the
1590s.

De Houtman's catalogue of 1598 called the pattern *De Vlieghe*, the fly, but when Johann
Bayer put it in his atlas of 1603 he labelled it Apis, the bee — a name it kept for the next
two centuries. Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille, mapping the southern sky from the Cape of Good Hope,
went back to the fly: *la Mouche* on his planisphere of 1756. Because there was already a
Northern Fly among the stars of Aries, he later Latinised it as Musca Australis, the
Southern Fly. The northern fly was eventually dropped, leaving Musca with the name to
itself.

## Notable stars

- **Alpha Muscae** — the brightest star of the fly, magnitude 2.7, a hot blue star about
  306 light-years away.
- **Beta Muscae** — magnitude 3.1 and some 311 light-years off, another blue star and a
  close double for telescope owners.
- **Delta Muscae** — at 91 light-years the nearest of the group, and distinctly orange
  beside its blue-white neighbours.
- **Epsilon Muscae** — a cool red giant, faint at magnitude 4.1 but a pleasing colour
  contrast in binoculars.

## How to find it

Musca is a Southern Hemisphere constellation, best placed on evenings around May. Find
Crux, the Southern Cross, then look just beyond its foot: Musca lies a little south of
Acrux, the Cross's brightest star, close to where the long axis of the Cross points.

The fly is a small lopsided quadrilateral of fourth- and third-magnitude stars. It is not
bright, but in that crowded stretch of the southern Milky Way it is easy to pick out once
you have the Cross to anchor you.
