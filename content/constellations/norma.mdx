## The Carpenter's Square

Norma is a small, faint constellation set in a rich stretch of the southern Milky Way. It
has no bright stars and no mythology of its own — but it sits in one of the most crowded
star fields in the sky, and a sweep with binoculars rewards the patient.

## History

Norma was invented by the French astronomer Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille, who catalogued the
southern sky from the Cape of Good Hope in 1751 and 1752. Like most of his additions, it
honours an instrument of science and craft rather than a god or hero: he called it
*l'Équerre et la Règle*, the set square and ruler, the tools of a draughtsman or carpenter.
The name was later shortened to Norma, Latin for a carpenter's square.

The constellation has one odd quirk: it has no Alpha or Beta star. Lacaille drew his new
figures into gaps between the older constellations, and the stars that would have carried
those letters were later handed back to neighbouring Scorpius. Norma's brightest star is
therefore Gamma.

## Notable stars

- **Gamma² Normae** — the constellation's brightest star at magnitude 4.0, a yellow giant
  about 128 light-years away. Its near neighbour on the sky, Gamma¹ Normae, is a far more
  distant star that happens to lie in the same direction, some 1,400 light-years off.
- **Epsilon Normae** — a hot blue star of magnitude 4.5, around 400 light-years away.
- **Mu Normae** — only magnitude 4.9 to the eye, but it lies nearly 4,700 light-years away
  and burns at some 30,000 K. To look that bright from so far, it must be one of the most
  luminous stars within reach of the naked eye.

## How to find it

Norma is a Southern Hemisphere constellation, best seen on July evenings. Start from
Scorpius and its red heart Antares, then look south, below the scorpion's body; Norma
lies there, wedged between Scorpius, Lupus, Ara and the small triangle of Triangulum
Australe.

Do not expect a shape to leap out — none of its stars is brighter than fourth magnitude,
and from northern latitudes it barely clears the horizon. Instead, treat it as a
destination for binoculars: the Milky Way runs straight through Norma, and the whole field
glitters with faint stars and small clusters.
