## The Octant

Octans is a faint scattering of stars at the very bottom of the sky, containing the south
celestial pole itself. It has no bright stars and no bold shape — but it holds the point
around which the entire southern sky turns.

## History

Octans carries no myth. It was invented by the French astronomer Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille
during his stay at the Cape of Good Hope in 1751–52, when he catalogued the far southern
stars and filled the blank spaces between them with fourteen new constellations. Nearly all
of them honour the instruments of science and craft: the telescope, the microscope, the
air pump, the sculptor's studio.

The octant is a navigator's instrument for measuring the angle between a star and the
horizon, a predecessor of the sextant. Lacaille's choice was apt — he placed it around the
south celestial pole, the very point a navigator would want to find.

## Notable stars

- **Nu Octantis** — the constellation's brightest star at magnitude 3.8, a yellowish
  star about 69 light-years away, and the one most easily seen from a dark site.
- **Beta Octantis** — magnitude 4.2, a hot white star roughly 140 light-years off.
- **Delta Octantis** — a cooler orange star of magnitude 4.3, about 279 light-years away.
- **Polaris Australis** — the southern pole star, sitting about a degree from the south
  celestial pole. At magnitude 5.5 and some 270 light-years away, it is barely visible to
  the naked eye, and far too dim to be the navigational anchor that Polaris is in the
  north.

## How to find it

Octans is a Southern Hemisphere constellation, and from most of the north it never rises at
all. Look low toward the south on a September evening, when it stands highest in the early
night.

The easiest route is to borrow the pointers that southern navigators have always used:
follow the long axis of the Southern Cross away from the Cross, and the dim field of stars
you eventually reach is Octans. Expect a quiet patch of sky — this is a constellation you
find by knowing where the pole is, not by spotting it.
