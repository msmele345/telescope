## The Serpent Bearer

Ophiuchus is a large constellation straddling the celestial equator, showing a man gripping
a serpent that winds through his hands. The Sun passes through it each December, which
makes it the famous "thirteenth" constellation of the zodiac — though astrologers have
never counted it.

## Mythology

The Greeks identified these stars with Asclepius, the greatest physician of the ancient
world. He learned his art from the centaur Chiron, and the secret of resurrection from a
snake: watching one serpent revive another with a herb, he took the herb for himself and
began raising the dead.

This upset the balance of things. Hades complained to Zeus that the underworld was being
emptied, and Zeus struck Asclepius down with a thunderbolt — then, regretting the loss of
so skilled a healer, placed him among the stars with his serpent still in his grasp. The
snake remains the emblem of medicine today, coiled around the rod of Asclepius.

## Notable stars

- **Rasalhague** — "the head of the serpent charmer," the brightest star of the
  constellation at magnitude 2.1 and a near neighbour at only 47 light-years away.
- **Sabik** — the second brightest, a hot white star about 84 light-years from us. Its name
  means "the preceding one."
- **Yed Prior** — a cool red giant about 170 light-years away, forming the hand that holds
  the serpent's head.
- **Yed Posterior** — the fainter companion to Yed Prior in the same hand; the two names
  simply mean the leading and trailing "hand" stars.
- **Zeta Ophiuchi** — a runaway star of blazing 33,000 K, flung out of its birth cluster
  and now ploughing through interstellar gas some 458 light-years away.
- **Cebalrai** — "the shepherd's dog," an orange giant at about 82 light-years, close to
  Rasalhague in the sky.

## How to find it

Look south on a July evening from the Northern Hemisphere, or high overhead in the
Southern Hemisphere's winter sky. Ophiuchus sits directly above Scorpius: find the red
star Antares, then look up and you are in the serpent bearer's territory.

Rasalhague is the easiest anchor — a lone bright star roughly midway between Vega and
Antares, just west of the Summer Triangle. From there the constellation spreads downward in a broad, rather faint kite,
with the body of the serpent trailing off to either side.
