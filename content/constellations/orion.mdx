## The Hunter

Orion is the most recognizable constellation in the winter sky — a giant hunter striding
across the heavens with a glittering belt of three blue-white stars at his waist.

## Mythology

In Greek myth, Orion was a giant huntsman of unmatched skill. The stories about him diverge
wildly: in some he is a companion of Artemis, the goddess of the hunt; in others he boasts
he can kill any creature on Earth, and the gods send a scorpion to humble him. The scorpion
stings him fatally, and Zeus places both into the sky on opposite sides — when Scorpius
rises in the east, Orion sinks in the west, fleeing from his old foe.

Orion's other myths reach much further back. In ancient Egypt, the same pattern of stars
was identified with Osiris, god of the afterlife. The pyramids of Giza are aligned with
Orion's belt — the pharaohs believed their souls would travel up its three stars to join
him.

## Notable stars

- **Betelgeuse** — the red supergiant marking Orion's right shoulder. So large that if you
  placed it where the Sun is, it would swallow the orbit of Jupiter. It is also a star
  near the end of its life, expected to go supernova on a cosmic timescale (anywhere from
  tomorrow to a hundred thousand years from now).
- **Rigel** — Orion's left foot, a brilliant blue-white supergiant about 860 light-years
  away.
- **The Belt** — Alnitak, Alnilam, and Mintaka. A nearly straight line of three hot blue
  stars, each one of the brightest in the sky.

## How to find it

In the Northern Hemisphere, look south on a winter evening. Orion's belt is the easiest
asterism in the sky to spot: three bright stars in a tidy row. Once you find the belt, the
rest of the constellation falls into place around it — Betelgeuse glowing red above and to
the left, Rigel blue-white below and to the right.

Following the belt down and to the left points you to Sirius, the brightest star in the
sky. Following it up and to the right takes you to the orange Aldebaran, the eye of Taurus.
