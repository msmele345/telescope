## The Peacock

Pavo is a far-southern constellation representing a peacock, its stars spread across a
region of sky that never rises for most northern observers. One bright star anchors it —
and that star is simply called Peacock.

## History

Pavo is not an ancient figure. It was introduced at the end of the sixteenth century by
the Dutch navigators Pieter Dirkszoon Keyser and Frederick de Houtman, who catalogued the
southern stars during voyages to the East Indies, and it appeared on a celestial globe of
1598 and then in Johann Bayer's star atlas of 1603, alongside the other "southern birds" —
Tucana the toucan, Grus the crane and Phoenix.

The name of its brightest star is more recent still. Alpha Pavonis was left without a
proper name for centuries until Britain's Royal Air Force asked for one in the 1930s, for
a navigation almanac that required every bright star to have a name. It became, plainly
enough, Peacock.

## Notable stars

- **Peacock** — Alpha Pavonis, magnitude 1.94 and about 183 light-years away. A searingly
  hot blue star, around 26,000 K at the surface, and far brighter than anything else in
  the constellation.
- **Delta Pavonis** — magnitude 3.56 and just 20 light-years from us, one of the closest
  naked-eye stars. It is a near twin of our Sun, slightly cooler and older, and a
  perennial favourite on lists of nearby stars worth searching for planets.
- **Gamma Pavonis** — magnitude 4.22 and about 30 light-years away, another Sun-like
  neighbour hiding in plain sight among much more distant stars.
- **Beta Pavonis** — magnitude 3.42 at roughly 138 light-years, a white star marking the
  other end of the peacock's body from Alpha.

## How to find it

Pavo is a Southern Hemisphere constellation, best placed on evenings around August, when
it rides high in the southern sky. From Europe and most of North America it never clears
the horizon at all; it only creeps above it south of about 30°N.

Start from the Southern Cross and follow the Milky Way east past Triangulum Australe, the
small bright triangle, and keep going. Peacock lies beyond it, a lone bright star in a
fairly empty stretch of sky, with the fainter stars of the bird spread to either side.
