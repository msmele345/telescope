## The Firebird

Phoenix is a modest constellation of the far southern sky, named for the mythical bird that
burns and rises again from its own ashes. It has one reasonably bright star and a scattering
of fainter ones, forming a shape that takes some imagination to read as a bird.

## History

Phoenix is one of twelve constellations introduced by the Dutch astronomers Pieter
Dirkszoon Keyser and Frederick de Houtman, who charted the southern sky during a trading
voyage to the East Indies in the 1590s. Their observations were engraved onto a celestial
globe by Petrus Plancius and published in Johann Bayer's great star atlas *Uranometria* in
1603, which is where most stargazers first met the new southern figures.

The twelve new constellations were largely exotic animals — a toucan, a flying fish, a
chameleon, a peacock — reflecting the wonders the voyagers had seen. Phoenix is the odd one
out, a creature from legend rather than from the Indies. Arabic and Chinese astronomers had
also grouped some of these stars into bird figures, but the constellation as we use it
today is a product of the age of European sea exploration, not of classical myth.

## Notable stars

- **Ankaa** — the brightest star in the constellation at magnitude 2.4, and the only one
  easy to pick out from a city. Its name comes from the Arabic for "the phoenix." It lies
  about 77 light-years away and shines with a warm yellow-orange light.
- **Beta Phoenicis** — magnitude 3.3 and about 200 light-years off, a close pair of
  yellowish stars that a small telescope can just separate.
- **Gamma Phoenicis** — a cool red giant at magnitude 3.4, some 234 light-years away; the
  reddest of the constellation's leading stars.
- **Zeta Phoenicis** — the hottest of the group, a blue-white star of magnitude 3.9 about
  280 light-years away. It is an eclipsing binary, dimming slightly as its two stars pass
  in front of one another.

## How to find it

Phoenix is best seen on November evenings, and really belongs to Southern Hemisphere
observers — from mid-northern latitudes it barely clears the horizon, if at all.

The easiest route is Achernar, the bright star at the end of Eridanus the River. Phoenix
lies just to its west, with Ankaa the brightest thing in that stretch of sky. Grus the Crane
sits further west again, so the two birds fly side by side.
