## The Painter's Easel

Pictor is a faint, unremarkable triangle of stars tucked between brilliant Canopus and the
Large Magellanic Cloud. Nothing in it is bright enough to catch the eye — yet one of its
stars changed how astronomers think about planets.

## History

Pictor is not an ancient figure and carries no myth. It was invented by the French
astronomer Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille, who spent two years at the Cape of Good Hope in the
1750s cataloguing the southern sky and filled its empty spaces with fourteen new
constellations, nearly all named after the instruments of art and science.

Lacaille called this one Equuleus Pictoris, "the painter's easel." Later astronomers
trimmed the name down to Pictor, which is why the constellation's name now means, strictly
speaking, "the painter" rather than the easel he worked at.

## Notable stars

- **Alpha Pictoris** — the constellation's brightest star at magnitude 3.3, a hot white
  star about 99 light-years away. It is the one member of Pictor that is reasonably easy
  to pick out with the unaided eye.
- **Beta Pictoris** — at magnitude 3.9 and 63 light-years away, it looks ordinary, but it
  is one of the most studied stars in the sky. It is young, and it is surrounded by a disc
  of dust and debris with at least two giant planets circling inside it — the first such
  disc ever imaged around another star.
- **Gamma Pictoris** — a cooler, orange-tinted star of magnitude 4.5, about 174
  light-years off, completing the constellation's narrow triangle.
- **Delta Pictoris** — fainter at magnitude 4.8 but far more distant, some 1,660
  light-years away, and one of the hottest stars here.

## How to find it

Pictor belongs to the Southern Hemisphere, riding high on summer evenings there around
January. Northern observers below about 35° latitude can glimpse it low in the south on a
winter night.

The way in is Canopus, the second-brightest star in the sky. Pictor lies immediately to
its west, a thin wedge of modest stars squeezed between Canopus and the bright keel of
Carina on one side and Columba on the other. Dark skies help: this is a constellation you
locate by its neighbours rather than by its own light.
