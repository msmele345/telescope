## The Fishes

Pisces is a faint, sprawling V-shaped constellation depicting two fishes connected by a
long cord. Despite holding the vernal equinox — the gateway from one celestial year to
the next — none of its stars are bright, and the constellation is among the harder zodiac
figures to find.

## Mythology

In Greek myth, Pisces represents Aphrodite and her son Eros. Fleeing from the monster
Typhon, the two leapt into the Euphrates river and transformed into fish to escape.
Some versions add the detail that they tied themselves together with a cord so they
would not lose each other in the dark waters — and that cord, knotted at the star
**Alrescha**, is still visible in the constellation today.

The two-fish figure is far older than the Greeks. Babylonian astronomy already showed
two fishes here, joined by a band, and ancient Egyptian and Syrian traditions also held
fish as sacred in this region of the sky.

Today Pisces holds an important and slowly drifting honor: the **vernal equinox** — the
point on the sky where the Sun crosses the celestial equator each March — currently lies
in Pisces. (Two thousand years ago it was in Aries, which is why Aries is still listed
as the "first" zodiac sign even though Pisces is.)

## Notable stars

- **Alrescha** — "the cord," a binary star marking the knot where the two fish are tied
  together. About 140 light-years away.
- **Eta Piscium** — the brightest star in Pisces, a yellow giant.
- **Van Maanen's Star** — not visible to the naked eye, but historically important: the
  third white dwarf ever discovered, a nearby (about 14 light-years) faint stellar
  remnant studied since 1917.

## How to find it

Pisces lies just south of the Great Square of Pegasus. Look for an elongated V or
shallow loop of dim stars trailing southwest from the Square's southwestern corner.
Best viewed in autumn from the Northern Hemisphere, when it sits high overhead in the
evening.

The fishes are dim — wait for a moonless night and a dark horizon if you want to trace
the full figure.
