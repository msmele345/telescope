## The Stern

Puppis is the stern of a ship — the largest surviving piece of Argo Navis, the great vessel
that once sprawled across the southern sky. It is a rich, crowded patch of the Milky Way,
scattered with hot blue stars and open clusters.

## Mythology

The ship is the Argo, built for Jason and the fifty heroes who sailed with him to Colchis
in search of the Golden Fleece. Among the crew were Heracles, Orpheus, and the twins Castor
and Pollux; the ship itself was said to speak, its prow carved from a sacred oak that could
give prophecy. When the voyage was over, Jason dedicated the Argo to the sea god Poseidon,
and it was set among the stars.

For centuries Argo Navis was charted as a single enormous constellation. In the 1750s the
French astronomer Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille divided it into three more manageable parts —
Carina the keel, Vela the sails, and Puppis the stern. The split explains a curiosity of
Puppis: it has no Alpha or Beta star, because those Greek letters went to the pieces of the
ship that sailed away with Carina.

## Notable stars and objects

- **Naos** — the brightest star of the stern, at magnitude 2.25, and one of the fiercest
  stars visible to the naked eye. Its surface burns at around 45,000 K, which gives it a
  hard blue-white glare; it lies some 1,400 light-years away, so its apparent brightness
  is hard-won.
- **Pi Puppis** — magnitude 2.70, an orange supergiant about 1,095 light-years off, cool
  at the surface but vast.
- **Tureis** — magnitude 2.81 and, at roughly 63 light-years, by far the closest of the
  bright stars here. A yellow-white star somewhat hotter than the Sun.
- **Tau Puppis** — magnitude 2.93, another orange star, about 183 light-years away.
- **M46 and M47** — two open clusters side by side, less than a degree apart. M47 is the
  brighter at magnitude 4.4 and can be glimpsed without optical aid on a dark night; M46,
  at magnitude 6.1, is fainter, more distant and far richer in stars.

## How to find it

Puppis lies best for Southern Hemisphere observers, high overhead on autumn evenings; from
northern latitudes it skims the southern horizon on spring nights in March.

Start from Sirius, the brightest star in the sky, and move south and east — away from
Orion. The Milky Way thickens there, and Puppis fills that glow, with Canopus in Carina
marking its far side. Sweep the region between Sirius and Naos with binoculars and you will
run into M46 and M47 as a matched pair of star clouds.
