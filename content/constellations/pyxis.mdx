## The Mariner's Compass

Pyxis is a small, faint constellation in the southern sky, representing the magnetic
compass of a ship. Its three brightest stars form a short, modest line — easy to overlook,
but part of one of the sky's great nautical scenes.

## History

Pyxis has no ancient myth behind it. It was created in the 1750s by the French astronomer
Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille, who catalogued the southern sky from the Cape of Good Hope and
filled its empty spaces with constellations named after instruments of science and
navigation — the clock, the telescope, the air pump, the compass.

Lacaille placed his compass alongside the stars of Argo Navis, the enormous ship of the
Argonauts, which he later divided into the keel, the stern and the sails (Carina, Puppis
and Vela). A mariner's compass was an anachronism aboard a Greek ship, but the idea stuck,
and Pyxis has sailed next to Puppis ever since.

## Notable stars

- **Alpha Pyxidis** — the brightest of the group at magnitude 3.7, a hot blue star of
  about 28,000 K lying some 845 light-years away.
- **Beta Pyxidis** — magnitude 4.0 and about 388 light-years off, a yellow star of roughly
  the Sun's colour.
- **Gamma Pyxidis** — an orange giant of magnitude 4.0, the nearest of the three at about
  209 light-years.
- **Theta Pyxidis** — fainter at magnitude 4.7, but the coolest and reddest star in the
  constellation, around 3,350 K.

## How to find it

Pyxis is best seen on March evenings, and much better placed for observers in the Southern
Hemisphere, where it rides high overhead. Northern viewers will find it low in the south.

Start from Sirius and follow the Milky Way southeast into Puppis, the stern of the ship.
Pyxis lies just east of Puppis, a short line of three modest stars with no bright
neighbours to confuse it. A dark sky helps — none of its stars reach third magnitude by
much, and from a city the compass simply vanishes.
