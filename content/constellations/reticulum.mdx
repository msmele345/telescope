## The Net

Reticulum is a small, faint diamond of stars in the far southern sky, named not for a hero
or a beast but for a piece of equipment: the crosshairs in an astronomer's eyepiece. It is
one of the quietest corners of the southern sky, and one of the most modern.

## History

The constellation was introduced in the 1750s by the French astronomer Nicolas-Louis de
Lacaille, who catalogued nearly ten thousand southern stars from the Cape of Good Hope.
Lacaille filled the gaps between the older southern figures with the instruments of science
and craft — a clock, a furnace, a microscope, a sculptor's studio. Reticulum honours the
*réticule*, the fine grid of wires in his telescope eyepiece that let him measure a star's
position precisely.

Lacaille was not quite the first to use these stars. A few decades earlier the Strasbourg
astronomer Isaak Habrecht II had marked out the same diamond as "Rhombus." Lacaille's name
stuck, and the International Astronomical Union kept it when the modern constellation
boundaries were drawn.

## Notable stars

- **Alpha Reticuli** — the brightest star in the net at magnitude 3.35, a yellow giant
  about 163 light-years away.
- **Beta Reticuli** — a cooler orange giant of magnitude 3.85, roughly 100 light-years
  from us.
- **Gamma Reticuli** — much more distant, some 490 light-years away, and distinctly red;
  at around 2900 K its surface is among the coolest of any star in the figure.
- **Zeta-2 Reticuli** — faint at magnitude 5.24, but a near neighbour at just 39
  light-years. It forms a wide pair with Zeta-1, and the two are often singled out as a
  rare example of twin Sun-like stars travelling together.

## How to find it

Reticulum is a Southern Hemisphere constellation, best seen on January evenings, and it
never rises for most northern observers. Start from Achernar, the bright star at the end of
Eridanus, and look south toward the Large Magellanic Cloud in Dorado. Reticulum lies
between them, a compact diamond no wider than a few finger-widths.

None of its stars are bright, so a dark sky helps. Once you have the diamond, you have the
whole constellation — there is little more to it than that neat small net.
