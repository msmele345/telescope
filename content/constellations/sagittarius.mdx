## The Archer

Sagittarius is a centaur drawing back his bow, aimed forever at the heart of Scorpius
just to the west. To modern eyes, the brightest stars of Sagittarius form an asterism
called the **Teapot**, complete with handle, lid, and spout.

## Mythology

In Greek myth, Sagittarius is most often identified with Chiron, the wise and noble
centaur who tutored heroes including Achilles, Jason, and Heracles. Chiron was accidentally
poisoned by one of Heracles' arrows and chose to give up his immortality, ending his
suffering. Zeus placed him in the heavens as Sagittarius.

(Confusingly, there is *another* centaur in the sky — the constellation Centaurus — and
Greek tradition is divided on which is Chiron.)

The constellation's most important feature has nothing to do with mythology. The center
of our Milky Way galaxy lies in Sagittarius, hidden behind dense clouds of dust. The
supermassive black hole at the galactic core, Sagittarius A*, is the gravitational anchor
around which our entire galaxy turns.

## Notable stars and objects

- **Kaus Australis** — "the southern bow," the brightest star in the Teapot, marking the
  bottom of the archer's bow.
- **Nunki** — a hot blue star that helped form the Teapot's handle.
- **The Milky Way's core** — on a dark night, the brightest, densest part of the Milky
  Way appears to rise out of the Teapot's spout like steam.
- **The Lagoon Nebula** (M8) and **the Trifid Nebula** (M20) — two glowing star-forming
  regions in Sagittarius, both visible in binoculars.

## How to find it

In the Northern Hemisphere, Sagittarius is best seen in summer evenings, low in the south.
Look for the **Teapot** — once you see the asterism, you cannot un-see it. The lid points
nearly straight up; the spout aims to the right. The handle is on the left.

Sagittarius is a treasure of summer skies: dark-sky observers point binoculars at it for
hours and find a new cluster, nebula, or star cloud at every shift.
