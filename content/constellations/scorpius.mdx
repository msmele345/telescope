## The Scorpion

Scorpius is one of the few constellations that genuinely resembles its namesake — a long,
curving tail of stars ending in a pair of bright stingers, with a pincer-bearing head and
a glowing red heart.

## Mythology

In Greek myth, Scorpius is the giant scorpion sent by the gods (or by Gaia, or by
Artemis, depending on the version) to kill the boastful hunter Orion. The scorpion stung
Orion fatally, and both were placed in the sky on opposite sides of the heavens — when
Scorpius rises in the east, Orion sinks in the west, fleeing his old foe forever.

Older still are the Sumerian and Babylonian scorpion-myths. In the *Epic of Gilgamesh*,
giant scorpion-people guard the gates of the Sun's daily journey through the underworld.
The same constellation marked the autumn equinox in those ancient calendars and was the
stinging end of summer.

## Notable stars

- **Antares** — the "rival of Mars," a red supergiant that shines with the same fiery
  hue as the planet Mars and is sometimes mistaken for it. About 550 light-years away,
  Antares is so large that, placed where the Sun is, it would extend nearly to Jupiter.
  Like Betelgeuse, it is in the late stages of its life.
- **Shaula** — "the raised tail," the bright stinger of the scorpion.
- **The Cat's Eyes** — the pair of stars Lambda and Upsilon Scorpii at the very end of
  the tail, glittering side by side.

## How to find it

In the Northern Hemisphere, Scorpius hugs the southern horizon in summer evenings —
look due south. Antares is unmistakable: a bright red star low in the south during July
and August. From Antares, the scorpion's body curves down and to the left, ending in
the upturned stinger.

Scorpius runs along the brightest part of the Milky Way, so on dark nights the scorpion
appears to swim in a river of stars.
