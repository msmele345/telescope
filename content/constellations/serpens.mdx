## The Serpent

Serpens is the only constellation split into two separate pieces of sky — the head
(Serpens Caput) to the west and the tail (Serpens Cauda) to the east, with Ophiuchus the
Serpent Bearer standing between them, gripping the snake in both hands.

## Mythology

The serpent belongs to Asclepius, the great healer of Greek myth, who learned the secret
of medicine by watching one snake bring herbs to revive another. Snakes shed their skins
and seem to be reborn, and so became the emblem of healing — the reason a serpent still
coils around the physician's staff today.

Asclepius grew so skilled that he began raising the dead, which upset the order of things.
Hades complained that his realm was emptying, and Zeus struck Asclepius down with a
thunderbolt — then placed him in the sky with his serpent in hand, where the two have
remained ever since.

## Notable stars and objects

- **Unukalhai** — the brightest star of the constellation, marking the serpent's heart;
  its name means "neck of the snake." A cool orange giant about 73 light-years away,
  shining at magnitude 2.65.
- **Eta Serpentis** — the second-brightest star, in the tail, an orange giant some 62
  light-years off at magnitude 3.26.
- **Alya** — a faint star marking the very tip of the tail, about 132 light-years away. It
  is a wide double, easily split in a small telescope.
- **M5** — one of the finest globular clusters in the sky, a tight ball of ancient
  stars. At magnitude 5.6 it is a hazy spot in binoculars and a glittering swarm in a
  telescope.
- **The Eagle Nebula (M16)** — a star-forming cloud in the tail at magnitude 6.0, home to
  the towering columns of gas that the Hubble telescope made famous as the Pillars of
  Creation.

## How to find it

Look south on a July evening; Serpens straddles the celestial equator, so it is available
from both hemispheres. Find the large figure of Ophiuchus first, then look to its
west for the head — a small triangle of stars with Unukalhai just below it, south of the
arc of Corona Borealis and north of Libra.

The tail runs east from Ophiuchus toward the Milky Way and the small constellation of
Scutum. Sweep that stretch with binoculars on a dark night: M5 sits about eight degrees
southwest of Unukalhai, and M16 lies in the star fields of the tail.
