## The Sextant

Sextans is one of the faintest constellations in the sky — a handful of dim stars in an
empty patch between Leo and Hydra. It commemorates not a hero or a beast but an
astronomical instrument.

## History

The constellation was introduced by the Polish astronomer Johannes Hevelius, who called
it Sextans Uraniae, "the sextant of Urania," the muse of astronomy, in a star atlas dated
1687 — the year he died, three years before his widow finished publishing it. Hevelius had spent decades measuring star positions from his rooftop
observatory in Danzig with a large sextant, insisting on naked-eye sighting long after
other astronomers had adopted telescopic sights.

In 1679 a fire destroyed his observatory, his instruments and much of his unpublished work.
Naming this quiet stretch of sky after the lost sextant was his memorial to it. There is no
myth attached to these stars — they were empty sky until Hevelius filled them, which is
part of why the figure is so faint.

## Notable stars

- **Alpha Sextantis** — the brightest star of the constellation at magnitude 4.5, and still
  only just visible from a dark site. It is a white giant about 287 light-years away.
- **Gamma Sextantis** — magnitude 5.1, a hot white star some 262 light-years distant. It is
  really two stars so close together that only a large amateur telescope can split them.
- **Beta Sextantis** — the hottest of the group, a blue-white star of magnitude 5.1 about
  345 light-years away, suspected of varying very slightly in brightness.
- **Epsilon Sextantis** — at roughly 183 light-years, the nearest of the constellation's
  brightest stars, a yellow-white point of magnitude 5.2.

## How to find it

Sextans straddles the celestial equator, so it can be seen from both hemispheres. Look for
it on April evenings — spring in the north, autumn in the south.

Use the brighter constellations on either side. Find Regulus, the bright star marking the
heart of Leo, then look south toward Alphard, the solitary orange star marking the heart
of Hydra. Sextans fills the gap below Regulus, just east of Alphard. You will need a dark, moonless sky: none of
its stars reach fourth magnitude, and under city lights the sextant disappears entirely.
