## The Toucan

Tucana is a modest constellation of the far southern sky, named for the big-billed bird of
South America. None of its stars is especially bright, but the figure lies in a quiet
corner of the heavens near the south celestial pole.

## History

Tucana has no classical myth. It is one of twelve southern constellations drawn from the
observations of the Dutch navigators Pieter Dirkszoon Keyser and Frederick de Houtman, who
charted the unfamiliar stars of the southern sky during a trading voyage to the East Indies
in the 1590s. The cartographer Petrus Plancius engraved them on a celestial globe in 1598,
and Johann Bayer included them in his great atlas *Uranometria* of 1603, which fixed them
in the sky for good.

Several of these new figures were exotic birds and animals inspired by the same voyages
— the toucan, the peacock, the crane, the bird of paradise, the flying fish. Tucana belongs
to the "Southern Birds," along with Pavo, Grus and Phoenix, a small menagerie of the Age of
Exploration that has stayed on our charts ever since.

## Notable stars and objects

- **Alpha Tucanae** — the constellation's brightest star at magnitude 2.9, a cooler orange
  giant about 199 light-years away. It marks the toucan's head.
- **Gamma Tucanae** — a yellow-white star of magnitude 4.0, and one of the nearer stars of
  the figure at about 72 light-years.
- **Zeta Tucanae** — at 28 light-years, practically a neighbour. It is a Sun-like star,
  slightly hotter and whiter than our own, shining at magnitude 4.2.
- **Beta Tucanae** — a group of six stars, of which the brightest two, Beta-1 and Beta-2,
  form a wide pair that a small telescope splits easily. Beta-1 is a hot blue-white star
  about 140 light-years off at magnitude 4.4.
- **Nu Tucanae** — the faintest star on this list at magnitude 4.8, and much the coolest: a
  deep red giant some 273 light-years away, two degrees southeast of Alpha.
- **47 Tucanae and the Small Magellanic Cloud** — the constellation's real prizes. 47
  Tucanae is one of the brightest globular clusters in the sky, and Tucana holds most of
  the Small Magellanic Cloud, a dwarf galaxy that orbits our own.

## How to find it

Tucana is a Southern Hemisphere constellation, best seen on November evenings — spring in
the south — when it stands highest after dark, close to the south celestial pole. It
never rises north of about 30°N, so most of Europe and North America will never see it.

Start from Achernar, the brilliant star at the end of Eridanus, and look south and west
toward the pole. Tucana's scattered stars lie between Achernar and the bright star Alnair
in Grus, the Crane. Expect a loose, faint pattern rather than an obvious bird: this is a
constellation you find by knowing where it should be. The easiest landmark is the Small
Magellanic Cloud, a pale patch like a detached piece of the Milky Way, with 47 Tucanae
glowing beside it as a fuzzy star.
