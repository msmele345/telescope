## The Great Bear

Ursa Major is one of the oldest and most universally recognized constellations. The seven
brightest stars form the Big Dipper — a familiar ladle hanging in the northern sky.

## Mythology

In the Greek tradition, Ursa Major is Callisto, a nymph and follower of Artemis who was
seduced by Zeus. Hera, jealous, transformed her into a bear. Years later Callisto's son
Arcas, now a hunter, came upon the bear in the forest. Before he could kill his own
mother, Zeus swept them both into the sky — Callisto as Ursa Major and Arcas as Ursa Minor,
their tails stretched long by the force of being lifted by the heavens.

The bear story is striking for how widely it spreads. Native American traditions, ancient
Siberian peoples, and many other cultures independently saw a bear in the same stars,
suggesting the figure may date back tens of thousands of years to a shared prehistoric
sky-myth.

## Notable stars

- **Mizar and Alcor** — the second star from the end of the Dipper's handle is actually
  two stars close together. Spotting both with the naked eye was a vision test in many
  cultures, called "the horse and rider" by the Arabs.
- **Dubhe and Merak** — the two outer stars of the Dipper's bowl. Drawing a line from
  Merak through Dubhe and extending it about five times that distance lands you on
  Polaris, the North Star.

## How to find it

In the Northern Hemisphere, the Big Dipper is visible year-round (it is *circumpolar* — it
never sets). Look north. In spring it stands high overhead with its handle hanging down;
in autumn it sits low on the horizon, scooping upward. Once you have the Dipper, you have
your bearings: it points to Polaris, and Polaris points to true north.
