## The Flying Fish

Volans is a small, faint constellation tucked beneath the keel of the great ship Carina,
deep in the southern sky. It represents a flying fish — one of the tropical fish that leap
from the water and glide above the waves, a sight familiar to sailors crossing the
southern oceans.

## History

Volans has no ancient myth behind it. Its stars were too far south to be seen from Greece,
Babylon or Egypt, and they went uncharted until European ships began making long voyages
into the southern hemisphere.

The constellation was devised in the 1590s by the Dutch navigators Pieter Dirkszoon Keyser
and Frederick de Houtman, who catalogued the southern stars during a trading expedition to
the East Indies. It was one of twelve new figures they introduced, most of them named for
exotic animals the crews encountered on the voyage. The German astronomer Johann Bayer
engraved it on his star atlas of 1603 as *Piscis Volans*, "the flying fish," and the name
was later shortened to simply Volans. Its placement is a small joke of the southern sky:
the fish appears to be flying away from Carina, the ship's keel, just as real flying fish
burst out of the water ahead of a bow.

## Notable stars

- **Beta Volantis** — the brightest star of the fish, an orange giant of magnitude 3.8,
  about 108 light-years away.
- **Gamma² Volantis** — a hair fainter at magnitude 3.8 and some 142 light-years off. It is
  the brighter half of a lovely colour-contrasting double star, easily split in a small
  telescope.
- **Alpha Volantis** — despite its name, only the fifth brightest here at magnitude 4.0.
  It is a hot white star about 124 light-years away.
- **Delta Volantis** — a yellow-white star of magnitude 4.0, and much more distant than the
  rest at roughly 660 light-years.

## How to find it

Volans is a southern constellation, circumpolar from most of the Southern Hemisphere and
essentially invisible from Europe and North America. Look for it high on autumn evenings
from the south — around March — though it can be traced for much of the year.

The easiest route is through Carina. Find brilliant Canopus, the second brightest star in
the sky, then follow the body of the ship south-east toward the Large Magellanic Cloud.
Volans lies in the gap between them, a modest scattering of fourth-magnitude stars. Wait
for a dark, moonless night: nothing in this constellation shines brightly, and city lights
will erase the fish entirely.
